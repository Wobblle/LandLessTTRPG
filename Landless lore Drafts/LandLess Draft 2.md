## CHAPTER 1: PORT DUNDY

A few miles out from the port, Brox climbs down to the lower deck. Midday sun to near pitch-black in four steps. He hits the first stair wrong and the servo in his right knee clicks loud enough that a passenger above him swears at the noise. He does not apologize. He just keeps going.

The underbelly of the ship is a throat. Machine lathes. Table saws bolted to the floor so they do not walk in a swell. The air tastes like wet rust and old coolant. Brox shoulders past a stack of empty crates and down between two giant water tanks, where the hull curves in and the dark gets honest.

Griselda is there. Folded up against the steel, one clawed hand hooked in a pipe so the roll of the ship cannot dump her onto the grating. Her green skin looks almost black in this light. The broken horn on her forehead catches a thin stripe of leak-light from a seam above. She has been down here the whole trip. She always is, this far south. The sun does not like her, and she does not like the sun, and both of them are too stubborn to negotiate.

Brox leans in and rubs the edge of her ear with the two fingers he still has that are meat. He sets a crispybar on her chest and a sunshake against her hip.

"A few miles to Dundy," he says.

Grisel rubs her eyes with the heel of her hand. The fans in Brox's mask are already spinning faster from the climb. She can hear them. She always hears them before she sees him. She sits up, cracks the shake, and drains half of it in one go. Then she leans in and gives him a quick kiss on the filter grill, which is as close as she gets to his mouth in public, or in private, or ever.

"I just hope he can find us a room this time," she says. "I mean a place where I can actually sleep. Not wake up every five minutes to some crash, or some scream, or some idiot dropping a wrench on a tank two feet from my head."

"That last one was me."

"I know it was you. I'm being generous."

Brox's voice comes out of the vocalizer flat and scraped. "Eigor got in ahead of us. He's holed up a few blocks off the import wing."

"Of course he is." Grisel finishes the shake and wipes her mouth with the back of her wrist. "Eigor arrives. Eigor finds a hole. Eigor texts us like we should be grateful he remembered we exist."

"He remembered the drinks."

"He remembered the drinks because he wants something." She looks at him. Really looks. The burlap-like filter mask, the twin intake fans over the eyes, the central grill that hisses every time he takes a breath he cannot take on his own. "You running hot?"

"Humidity."

"You always say humidity."

"It's always humid."

She lets it go. She unwraps the crispybar, bites it, and talks around it. "If he got us a room with a door that closes, I will kiss him on the mouth. Bark and all."

"I'll tell him."

"Don't you dare."

They stay down there until the ship kisses the dock and the rest of the passengers thump off in a herd. Grisel has avoided the sun for most of the trip already. She is holding off getting blinded for as long as the hull will let her. When the deck above goes quiet, Brox grabs his backpack and carry case. Grisel takes her carry case and the little purse she insists is not a purse. They walk toward the exit. She covers her eyes with one hand and keeps the other on the back of Brox's coat.

They step out onto the deck and the south hits her like a sheet of heated glass. She stops walking. Brox does not let her stop walking.

"I really can't see a thing," Grisel says. "Please. I feel like I could fall right into—"

"I got you." His mechanical left hand finds her elbow and stays there. "It's been a long time since you have been this far south."

"I remember why."

"You remember the hangover. You don't remember the sun."

"I remember both. The hangover had the decency to end."

They start down the boardwalk into the entrance of Port Dundy, one of the few neutral metropolises left in the hemisphere. Every kind of animal walks the city like it has a shift. A Howling with giraffe-stretch in the neck is selling filtered water out of a drum. A pair of baseline kids chase a rat the size of a dog and the rat is winning. A Timberite cohort moves through the crowd in a tight, silent knot, bark-skin dull as old furniture, not looking at anyone they do not have to look at.

Dundy is divided into neighborhoods the way a wreck is divided into rooms: by whoever got there first and nailed something down. It has a reputation for all the little nooks and cracks where anyone can fall through. Sometimes literally. Keel-hauled and dragged under the old slabs. Other times they just go missing, which is the polite way of saying the water took a vote.

Like most of the remaining settlements on Earth, Dundy is a massive stretch of floating rafts and slabs. Some of it is historic, which here means the wood is older than the people standing on it and nobody has been stupid enough to replace it. The city holds a steady two million. About sixty percent are permanent residents, which is high. The rest are traders, drifters, runners, schemers, soldiers, or anyone who lives the way of the open water and calls it a choice.

Brox, Grisel, and Eigor are in the forty percent. They are not regulated traders. They are not here on vacation. They are here for business, which is the word Eigor uses when he does not want to say job, and the word Brox uses when he does not want to say problem.

Eigor makes most of the money. He supports all three for the most part. Brox and Grisel take odd jobs when the odd jobs do not require a face people want to see, or lungs that work in open air, or a Howling walking into a shop without somebody reaching for a weapon. Eigor does contract work.

Eigor is a Timberite. Timberites have a long-refined, bio-engineered bark for skin. Smooth. Flexible. Nearly the same as the trees that surround a typical Timberite compound. They usually stick in cohorts. Private. Stoic. They do not explain themselves to water people, and water people have learned not to ask.

Eigor can be stoic. He can also drink a bar dry and call it research. He and Brox belong to no company. The only reason the Timberites still let Eigor take contracts is the rare expression. For every Timberite birth, there is a chance to receive the full neuro-hijacking trait. NH. Eigor has tendrils in his hand. If he can slice the back of someone's head, he can put those tendrils in and take the body. Motor control. A bleed of memory. The subject stays in there and watches their own hands do the work.

Somewhere down the line, his species tested a lot of traits. They wanted to be super. Most of it was considered failure. Then, every so often, someone got born with the full expression, and the failures became a feature. Eigor explained it once like this: rolling ten natural D20s at once. Brox told him that was a stupid way to explain it. Eigor said Brox was just mad he rolled a one on lungs.

They find him at an outdoor bar on the import-side edge, where the boardwalk is wide enough that a fight can happen without putting anyone in the water by accident. Eigor has a table already. He has a drink already. He has that look he gets when the last job is still sitting in his mouth and he has not decided if he is going to spit it out.

Brox drops his carry case and sits. The chair complains. Grisel sits beside him, still squinting, one hand up like a visor.

"You're late," Eigor says.

"We're on a passenger hauler," Brox says. "You're on whatever you stole."

"Rented."

"Sure."

Eigor sips. His bark-skin does not show sweat. It never does. "How was the hold?"

"Dark," Grisel says. "Which is the only compliment I have for this latitude. You get the room?"

"I got a lead on a room."

"That is not a room."

"It's a better sentence than no."

Brox leans forward. The fans over his eyes tick up a notch. "How'd the job go?"

Eigor looks at his drink like it might grow a door. "We can talk about that later."

"That's what you said last time."

"Last time later was later. This time later is still later."

"What we are doing next," Eigor says, after a beat, "is what you want to ask."

Brox sits back. "I know what we're doing next. The cleanest bed you can find. Right after we zap our brains for a little while."

Eigor lifts his glass. Brox claps it. They both throw it back. Grisel watches them the way she watches weather: not surprised, not impressed, already planning around it.

A few hours go by. A few dozen drinks go by among the three of them, which is not the same as a few dozen drinks each, but it is close enough that the distinction stops mattering. The bar fills with dock noise. Somebody two tables over loses at cards and starts explaining why the cards are wrong. A Howling with bear in the shoulders laughs so hard a mug goes off the table and nobody picks it up.

Brox is listing. Not falling. Listing. He jabs a finger at Eigor.

"You gonna pass out like them, you crusty shitstain?"

Eigor laughs and slightly spits his drink. "Not tonight. I'm pretty sure if I woke up I'd be lucky to have a shoe left." He turns the glass in his hand. "Let's go to the Daisy District."

Brox makes a sound that might be a laugh if the mask would let it be one. "We're both about to fall asleep. Why would I go anywhere near there? Especially with Grisel."

Grisel points at him without looking up from her drink. "Thanks, Eigor. But he's right. I'm sorry to tell you I would much rather get some rest than blow my ears out."

Eigor pulls two stims from his coat and sets them on the table like he is dealing a hand he already knows. "I wanted to talk to you about the job. And the next job. Ship leaves tonight, though." He taps the stims. "Just take these. Once we board, you have like twelve hours. I'll grab a bunk."

Grisel stares at the pills, then at him. "I hope you're joking. All of this is the opposite of what I was expecting right now."

"We need a loud place." Eigor leans in. The stoic cracks just enough to show the calculation underneath. "Hear me out, then decide what you want to do. I already have like eighty Glove. I'll toss you guys fifteen either way."

Brox's eyes, what can be seen of them behind the fans, do that rare thing they do. They widen. He hides it by drinking.

Grisel says, "Brox is already fried and I'm not even browned. You buy the drinks."

"Sure."

"And carry my stuff."

Eigor sighs. "Fine."

"And the room," Grisel adds. "After. A real one. Door. Lock. No wrench."

Eigor looks at Brox. "She always like this after a nap?"

"She's like this after a nap, before a nap, and during the part where she pretends she might nap." Brox picks up a stim, turns it over, and pockets it. "Daisy District. You talk. We listen. If the talk is stupid, I'm eating the stim and sleeping on your floor anyway."

"It's not stupid."

"You always say that too."

Eigor stands, collects Grisel's carry case without comment, and starts walking. The bark of his neck looks darker in the late light, like wet wood. Brox and Grisel follow him into the crowd, into the part of Dundy that does not care what you are as long as you can pay to be loud.

Behind them, the bar keeps going. In front of them, the boardwalk narrows, and the water under the slabs sounds closer, like it has been listening the whole time.

## HISTORICAL CONTEXT: THE NUCLEAR CAMPFIRE (2098)

*Recovered briefing, Freechron Internal / later circulated as public myth. Dates verified against plant-commission logs.*

The late twenty-first century solved energy the way it solved most problems: by building more of the thing that had already almost killed it. Advancements in nuclear plants made small-community reactors viable. A town could run its own fire. The benefits were obvious. The security problem was not.

Most of the risk got sanded down with efficiency, automation, and isolation. Reactors were put where people were not. Cameras watched the cameras. Still, there were tasks automation could not finish. A valve that had to be felt. A smell that was wrong before any sensor called it wrong. A human being who would fall asleep, or take a bribe, or decide the alarm was a drill.

In 2098, a lot of those concerns became a thing of the past.

The late 2100s had no shortage of bioengineering companies. Almost all of them were trying to improve the human body. Stronger bone. Cleaner blood. Longer life, if you could pay for the years. Only one company spent serious research on uplifting.

Freechron.

Freechron did not want a better human. Freechron wanted a better animal. Lifespan. Intelligence. Strength. Dexterity. Their first successful, replicable gene-set is what ended up solving most of the plant security problem, not because that was the goal, but because the goal and the problem happened to fit in the same mouth.

What became lovingly known as the Howlers started as a new type of dog.

They were smart enough to do the plant work. They were given thumbs. Their senses were pushed until a Howler could hear a hairline crack in a coolant line from the other side of a containment wall. They did not get bored in the way people got bored. They did not look at a reactor and see a career. They saw a fire that had to be watched.

Howlers were treated as authority almost immediately, and not because anyone voted on it. They were dedicated. They could not be bought with the usual currencies. Within a short time they were deployed into almost every plant that could afford the contract. Within a few years there was no need for any humans on site at all.

They could sense anyone inside the perimeter. They were the most trusted workers on the grid. They enjoyed the work. They got nothing but respect, which, for a while, was true enough to print.

It started to sound inevitable, the way people talk when they want a decision to feel like weather. Man's best friend should be the one watching the fire. The natural next evolution of the dog. A campfire story with a payroll.

What the public briefings left out was the part Freechron already knew: once you hand a species a job that important, and you make them better at it than you are, you do not get to take the job back. You only get to hope they still like you when the fire gets bigger than the camp.

## HISTORICAL CONTEXT: THE FIRST MESSAGE (2130)

*Compiled from open-frequency logs, Sol Broadcast Archive, and later Stillous-authorized translations. Popularly called First Message Day, though the message itself was not a day. It was a week of people pretending they had always expected it.*

Everyone can remember where they were when first contact was made. That is the sentence people use, and it is only half true. The message was a footnote in the tabloids one day. The next day everybody was talking like they had been waiting their whole lives to be spoken to.

The message was clear. Open. Anyone could read it.

They called themselves Stillous.

An alien race confirmed they were heading toward Earth on a generation ship that had already been traveling for twelve hundred years. It took a decade for them to even read the human response. Then nine years for Earth to hear them answer. For the next hundred years the delay got shorter and shorter, like a long conversation walking into the same room.

They were given a region of the moon. Their own land. Close enough to humans to trade and far enough to sleep. They came from a planet with tougher conditions and less atmosphere. Earth air was rich to them. Earth gravity was a suggestion they learned to live with.

When they departed their home, they did not know about humans. They did not expect us. They knew only that Earth showed the signs of a world that could hold life. They did not realize anyone was already here until around the 2050s. They spent time debating whether to turn back. They decided to make contact instead. They had plenty of years to learn the languages and the history before they said hello.

The Stillous and the humans were alike enough to make agreements, and excited enough to mean some of them. There were many small differences. A few major ones.

The strangest thing for humans was their recorded history.

The Stillous development from dormant and animalistic to advanced intelligence was all saved. It was not myth. It was footage, logs, tool-marks, the whole ugly staircase. Beside the Stillous, one other alien species existed in their oldest records. Some said that species was extinct. Some said it was very far away. The Stillous knew almost nothing about them except this: they were the ones who raised the entire Stillous civilization out of pre-tribal nature.

Ancient probes had landed on the Stillous homeworld with advanced uplifting tech. Early Stillous learned to work the terminals. They made tools they should not have been able to make. The probes dropped in the thousands. Most still existed and were still in use centuries later. Some had functions the Stillous had never learned. Throughout their existence, the origin of their intelligence was known and available. They could watch themselves get made.

They had assumed, reasonably, that whoever sent the probes had been lifted by someone older, and that someone by someone older still, a chain of raised hands going back to the first possible mind. Then they found humans.

Humans had done it alone.

That fact did more damage to Stillous philosophy than any weapon Earth owned. Where Stillous had certainty, humans had religion. Where Stillous had a recorded parent, humans had a gap and a thousand stories stuffed into it. The Stillous on the ship were in a strange position: they had come to pass along the torch, to find a living world and uplift whatever was there. They had thought of that as a sacrifice. A duty. Then they arrived and the animals were already building cities and lying about why.

The homeworld would not hear any of this for centuries, and would not be able to answer for centuries after that. The mission was not going to play out the way it was planned. The biggest advantage the travelers had was how excited and accepting the humans were. Excitement is a kind of blindness. The Stillous were polite enough not to call it that.

They were not one people. The ship held a full, messy slice of their cultures, the most adventurous of every kind. They came in all shapes and sizes, from a few feet tall to nearly eight, scaly skin in every pattern, some natural, some designed. Bipedal. Two arms. Many of them tall and slouchy by nature. They all had faces that looked, to humans, almost like a smile. They often said humans had a face that looked like we were always about to eat.

Their genders divided into two working categories: supplementive and maintenance. There were a few dozen forms of genitalia, six associated with the supplementive gender. In nature, supplementives provided nutrients to the colony. Builders. Hunters. Town security. Maintenance genders kept the newborns alive. They gave birth in cycles, in hut-like structures with a hard outer shell and inner incubation chambers. Some fed the body. Some maintained it directly. Others handled birth cycles and the long work of keeping an immobile infant from dying. Stillous babies could not move for a few years. They needed constant attention. They reached adulthood around thirty Earth years. Natural lifespan sat around two hundred. Their biology was tough and easy to modify. On the ship, many had already learned how to push that number until death became more of a decision than a date.

About eighty percent of the ship's population had been born in transit. They knew nothing of the homeworld except stories and archives. Most spent long stretches in stasis, taking turns waking for maintenance and child-rearing. About six hundred thousand total. Of the original one hundred sixty thousand that left, only around eleven thousand had died. Many by choice. A few by accident. The ship was huge and getting crowded. A lot of them opted to stay asleep.

They started giving Earth information and technology. Major advancements. One of them was an efficient, compact energy source: almost near-complete stable energy in its densest form.

They called it a Glove.

A small one could be picked up in a hand, if the hand did not mind a few volts. Like a battery, except it output a constant charge. They came in sizes from a dime to a small boat. A Glove would hold a consistent voltage for a few thousand years. They could be combined. They did not care if you used them to light a room or run a city.

Earth did not understand, not at first, that a gift that clean always has an exhaust. The Stillous understood. They just did not think the exhaust was their problem.

## CHAPTER 2: BOOTY CALL

In the year 2668, Gloves are the main premium currency and the prime energy source. The world is flooded, minus a few irradiated deserts that nobody calls land unless they are trying to sell it.

Eigor hands over fifteen Gloves to Brox in a glass sleeve, right there on the walk to Daisy, like he is afraid Brox will sober up enough to say no if he waits. Brox has very few facial expressions. His eyes still do the thing. They go wide and stay wide while he shoves the sleeve into his bag, half stumbling, suddenly a little more awake because money has that effect even when the lungs do not.

"We should go to that loud place and tell some stories then," Brox says.

Brox, like his lifelong friend Eigor, is part of an estranged faction of people. He did not get the abilities Eigor got. He cannot breathe without a filter mask. Typical of his kind.

The Rhombus.

A people many generations down from over-bio-experimenting. Brox is a halfway decent engineer and falls short just about anywhere else. He wears a long coat because the sun would eat his skin. Most Rhombus stay away from the normal population. Brox cannot stand his own people. He was not raised by them. He finds them annoying for the most part, which is a kind way of saying they look at a living body and see a prototype that has not been finished.

The three of them cut toward the Daisy District. Venues. Call girls. Music that starts in your teeth. Leaving the bar, they pass sleeping transients, shacks nailed to shacks, stretches of flooring where the wrong step is a thirty-foot drop into the dark water under the city. Dundy is sixteen miles of floating scrap. Rafts and huts and old civic slabs pretending they are still a plan. Like most of Earth in the 2600s, it is full of poverty and people who have learned to make poverty look like a skill. Some neighborhoods belong entirely to cohorts who claim the city as descendants of the original settlers, and they rival the other cohorts who think the same thing. It can be a great place to make money. It can be a great place to get stabbed in an alley. Sometimes those are the same afternoon.

Grisel walks with her hood up even though the sun is finally dropping. The broken horn still makes a shape under the fabric. A couple of dock kids stare. She stares back until they find something else to be brave about.

"You didn't have to flash the whole sleeve in the street," she tells Eigor.

"I flashed it to Brox. The street can mind its business."

"The street is made of people who mind other people's business for a living."

Brox adjusts the bag against his ribs. "If somebody wants to rob a Timberite, a Howling, and a man who sounds like a dying generator, they can try. I'll even hold the bag open."

"Don't joke that where I can hear it," Grisel says.

"You can hear everything."

"That's what I just said."

After twenty minutes they find a nightclub that is doing the most honest work in Daisy: making it too loud to think and too dark to judge. They walk in. They grab drinks. Eigor looks for a table. Grisel hears the start of a song she likes and her whole body changes, like somebody threw a switch in the spine. She throws her hands up, grabs Brox, and pulls him onto the floor, almost spilling his drink.

He smiles. The mask hides most of it. She can still tell. She always can. She shouts, holds one hand up, sips, and knocks her shoulder into his chassis hard enough that the servo complains.

Eigor appears at the edge of the floor with two more glasses and a face that says he is trying to be a person for five more minutes.

"That stim starting to kick in?"

"I'm wide awake now," Brox says.

"I'm glad and all," Eigor says, "but I needed you guys alert for a specific reason."

Brox leans in so he does not have to shout as much. "The fuck you want, man. We're having a good time."

Eigor leans closer. The bass makes his voice sound like it is coming up through the floor. "We have a full onyx war chest in route."

Brox goes still. The fans over his eyes jump. "Was that what your last job was?"

"We should go sit down then." Eigor glances at the bar. "Grab another drink first. Get loose for this one."

They find an empty table on the edge of the venue, the kind of table that exists so people can pretend they came here to talk. Grisel sits with her back to a pillar. Brox sits where he can see the door. Eigor sits like a man who has already counted the exits and did not like the number.

He does a double take at a pair of Onyx-looking coats near the bar, decides they are just men who want to look expensive, and leans in.

"So I'm doing an extraction job. We have a thing on one boat. It needs to be on another."

He tells it like he tells most things: from the middle, then backward, then the part that matters.

He was half deep into Timberite territory. Floating trees everywhere, the compounds sitting on their own green islands like they had never heard of the rest of the world. Up the way, an extraction crew was already in position. Eigor's job was the part nobody else could do. There was a chest in a room right against the hull. One Glove-embedded explosive. Maybe one guard. Maybe three. For that, for the professional use of the thing in his hand, they were going to pay him enough to take care of everyone for months.

"I did what I call fishing," Eigor says.

He ties a long rope to his foot. He sets a giant net in the path of the subject ship. The net grabs the hull. Eigor gets ripped under the water.

He says this the way another man might say he caught a cab.

He attaches to the ship. Climbs the rope toward the bottom hull. Climbs up the side to a storage room near the back. Laser-cuts a tiny hole, slips in, waits behind a crate. Ten minutes. A guard wanders close enough. The hole is already leaking. The guard comes to check it. Eigor slices the back of his head, grabs the open wound, and connects.

The club keeps thumping. Eigor's voice drops into that gravel he uses when the story stops being a story.

"I sent the signal I was on final approach. I sliced the guard, got in his head, and mounted his automated rifle. Outside the hull, cannons are going off. Missiles hitting the water. I force my way down the hall using the meat-shield, gun down four guys in Onyx gear, and blow the door."

He takes a slow breath. His dark eyes lock on Brox.

"Inside is a fully locked Onyx Empire war chest. Fully stacked. Had to weigh two hundred kilos. I shoved my explosive into the guard's mouth, kicked him off me, and he blew a hole right through the hull. I grabbed the chest strap as I fell backward. The extraction crew bridged themselves over the water and caught me."

He taps a finger on the sticky table.

"Half the crew on that ship were Onyx. The other half were everyday sea-slums. It was a tax collection, moving south. But when we hauled that chest onto the deck, I felt the weight of it. I've seen the lock mechanisms. It's securing clean, Earth-minted scripts." He lets the club noise fill the space he leaves. "Inside that chest is retire money. It's shuttle money."

Brox wipes his mouth beneath the filter mask, a habit that does not do anything and never has. "Who in the north would ever pay a tax to the Empire?" He holds up a hand before Eigor can answer. "Honestly, forget all of that, because I know where this is going. You don't just want your cut from the extraction crew. You want the whole chest."

Eigor does not deny it. That is how Brox knows he is right.

"They're in the side docks," Eigor says. "Leaving in four hours. A crew of twelve on a hefty boat. I need hands. This isn't a job where I can just come back to the bunk afterward. We take this, we're gone."

"The dirty docks?" Brox scoffs, and the vocalizer makes it sound worse. "Going up against a dozen Timbers? What do you need us for? What could I even do in four hours besides catch a nap?"

"Here's the thing," Eigor says. "I get word the ship was raided and taken by a small Onyx crew. There's no way they didn't recover the chest, and there's no way they won't be traveling the strait to get back on track."

Brox shakes his head. "Why do you act like this makes the situation any better?"

"I don't know. You agreed to hear the plan though."

"I agreed to hear the plan because you put fifteen Gloves in my bag and a stim in my blood. That is not the same as me being smart."

Eigor almost smiles. "I need someone to bypass the dock anchors while I handle the crew. And I need someone who can pilot a skiff fast enough to get us out of the sector before the Empire or the Timbers realize what happened."

Brox looks at his hands. One metal. One not. He looks over at Grisel. The silence between them is heavier than the music.

"I get it," Brox mutters. "But it doesn't matter how well I understand the tech. If I go down there, it's out of my hands. It's a coin toss." He reaches out and touches Grisel's arm, gentle, like the arm might break if he does it like he means it. "You can't come. You have to stay back."

Grisel stares at her drink. Neon crawls across the amber. She thinks about the endless damp of lower decks. The scraping noises outside a hull that keep her awake. The Breakers who took her family, dragging through the dark, the sound of it still living in her teeth. She looks at Brox. At the mask. At the fragile, exposed life they are calling a life. Shuttle money means off the water. Thick walls. A door that closes. A place where the dark does not have a current.

She swats his hand away. Her jaw is tight.

"Stay back where, Brox? Here? Waiting to see if you get your throat cut? Waiting to see if some transient kicks me off a thirty-foot drop while you're gone? I came south so I wouldn't sit in a hole and listen. Don't put me back in the hole and call it love."

Brox's fans stutter. "I didn't say love."

"You never say it. You just do that thing with your hand and hope I translate."

She looks at Eigor. Her hands are shaking. She forces them flat on the table.

"You really think this buys us a shuttle out of the wet?"

Eigor nods. Slow. "I do."

"Say it like a person."

"I think that chest is the first thing we've touched that isn't just another month. I think if we don't take it, somebody else will, and we will still be sleeping over water, and you will still be waking up every time a hull makes a noise." He turns his glass. "I also think if I do it alone I die, and then you two get to split my coat. So. Yes. I think it buys the shuttle."

Grisel swallows the knot in her throat. She is not a fighter, not the way they need a fighter, and she knows she is the weakest link at this table. She also knows the alternative. Waiting to die in the dark is still dying.

"I'm driving the skiff," she says. Her voice comes out hard, like she had to bake it that way. "And we split it even. Let's do it."

Brox starts to argue. She is already standing. Eigor is already counting the time they have left. The song changes. Nobody at the table hears the new one.

## ARCHIVAL FRAGMENT: THE IDEOLOGY SCHISM (2412)

*Excerpt, Sol Historical Survey, Volume VII: Out of Context Events. Circulated in lunar academies; banned in two Mirlyn cohorts; popular in Dundy bars as the thing people quote when they want to sound like they read.*

The timeline of Earth's collapse is not defined by a single war. Historians call the sequence the Out of Context Events, which is a polite way of saying the planet was asked a question it did not have the language to answer.

The Stillous generation ship did not arrive as conquerors. It arrived as indifferent benefactors. Their moral framework was born of extreme age and complete historical clarity. They knew exactly who had seeded their intelligence. They had outgrown religion, or at least they had outgrown the need to call it that. Sentimentality was a human habit they found interesting and nonessential. Earth, to the original vanguard, was a logistical waypoint. A planetary substrate. A place to stand while the next part of the mission got organized.

Then the Gloves went to work.

Stillous-gifted Glove plants reacted with Earth's atmospheric pressure and began outputting hyper-dense water vapor as a byproduct of near-infinite energy. The oceans rose. Not in a movie way. In a math way. Year by year, coast by coast, the map became a suggestion.

The Stillous did not intervene.

Their biology was resilient. Adaptable to harsh atmospheres and deep pressures. The flooding of a terrestrial world was a negligible variable in a macro-system. They had come to uplift, and the species they found was already intelligent, so the mission became maintenance, and maintenance did not include pumping a planet dry because the natives had built their cities too close to the tide.

This indifference birthed the Schism.

As decades passed, Stillous born in the Sol system — who had never seen Echidna, never stood under Typhon's light, never touched the old probes except as museum objects — began to fracture.

Traditionalists wanted to expand the clinical empire across the drowned Earth. Keep the mission. Keep the distance. Treat the water as a new floor.

Separatists, stained by human culture, rebelled and formed sympathetic enclaves. They learned jokes. They learned guilt. Some of them even learned to lie about why they were helping.

Others abandoned the grand mission entirely. They took Stillous tech into the open water and became piratical warlords, because a tool does not care what kind of hand holds it, and a Glove does not ask if you are building a hospital or a throne.

Humanity retreated as the continents went under. The nuclear plants and the Glove reactors were left to the Howlers. Freechron's uplifted dogs remained at their posts. They were the last line of defense against total ecological annihilation, and they knew it, because knowing it had been bred into them as surely as the thumbs.

When it became clear humanity could no longer protect the plants — or the Howlers — the Howlers took command of the remaining bio-engineering bays. They did not hold a summit. They did not send a letter. They opened the tanks and started working.

They synthesized their genome with other mammalian lines. Apes. Giraffes. Bears. They integrated cybernetics aggressively enough to survive a corrosive, flooded world that had never been designed for any of them.

By the 2600s they were no longer just the plant workers.

They were the Howlings.

A massive, wildly diverse faction of human-animal-machine hybrids. Fiercely neutral. Deeply loyal to their own. Engineered to endure the world the Stillous had accidentally broken, and uninterested in thanking anyone for the accident.

On Earth, people still told the campfire story. Man's best friend, watching the fire.

The Howlings told a different one. The fire got too big. The men left. The dogs stayed. Then the dogs made sure they would never have to ask permission to stay again.

## CHAPTER 3: THE DIRTY DOCKS

The Daisy District does not end so much as it loses the argument with gravity. Neon gives up. The industrial tier takes over with that sputtering sodium-orange that makes every face look already bruised. The air thickens: ozone, decaying kelp, the chemical slap of exhaust. Music becomes machinery. Machinery becomes the city's pulse, which is a generous word for a leak.

Grisel walks a few paces behind Eigor. Her heavy, clawed feet find the slick planks without looking. Even in the gloom her green skin holds a little light, a genetic marker she has never been able to wash off. She runs a hand over the broken horn, a nervous habit she would deny if anyone named it, and her other hand stays on the flail. The spiked iron ball ticks against the cybernetic plating fused into her right thigh. Clink. Clink. A clock that only she can hear.

She hates this part of Dundy. The lower levels are where people like her family went to disappear. Where the Breakers crawled out of the black and took the living down, and the city wrote it up as weather.

"Keep your head on a swivel," Eigor mutters. His bark-skin eats the alley shadow. "We're not hitting them from the boardwalk. I hired a cutter. Crew of drifters. They're already in slip twelve, waiting on a reason to be brave."

"You hired a crew before we said yes," Brox says.

"I hired a crew before I bought the drinks. The drinks were the polite part."

Brox trudges beside Grisel, long coat doing almost nothing to hide the pale-blue cybernetic chassis of his legs and torso. Every step has a faint servo whine. His face is gone behind the filter mask. Twin intake fans over the eyes. Central grill hissing. He sounds like a machine that learned how to be tired.

"You're running hot," Grisel says, catching the limp in the right piston-leg.

"Just the humidity." Brox reaches down with the robotic hand and tweaks a pressure valve at his waist. "And the fact that Eigor is marching us into a meat grinder he already paid a deposit on."

"We're the ones holding the grinder," Eigor says.

The mercenary cutter is an ugly, honest boat. Iron where wood would have been cheaper. Patches where iron got embarrassed. A dozen hardened drifters wait in the belly of it, a mix of scarred baseline humans and augmented scrappers, standing around a rusted plotting table like the table might bless them. The air is sour sweat, machine oil, ozone.

Eigor does not speak right away. He lets the engine hum do the talking. Then he takes out a glass sleeve and tosses it onto the metal.

Fifteen Gloves. The clack is louder than it should be.

The crew shifts. That much premium currency in one place can buy a small island in the northern archipelago, or at least the story of one.

"The Onyx Empire is moving a chest through the strait," Eigor says. Low. Resonant. "Tax collection. Clean Earth-minted scripts. Enough to retire every soul in this room."

A cyborg with a half-melted face steps in. "It's Onyx, Timber. Armor that eats standard ballistics. Guards that don't blink. We bring this bucket near their slip, they turn us into slag."

Eigor smiles. It is a slow, wrong stretch of wooden features. He raises his right hand. The bark of his palm splits with a wet sound and the pale neuro-tendrils come out, snapping and curling in the damp.

"I will handle the guards," he says. "Once I'm in their heads, their armor works for us."

The cyborg steps back. Disgust and greed do a little dance in the one good eye. "That gets you on the deck. What about the skiff's cannons? They'll tear our hull apart before you even make the jump."

Eigor looks past the table, into the shadows. "That's why we aren't using standard ballistics."

Brox steps into the light. The servos announce him. He does not look at the crew. He walks past the table to the cutter's main starboard cannon, a heavy archaic slug-thrower meant for punching rafts, not starting a war.

He pulls a single Glove from his coat.

The room goes dead in the way rooms go dead when somebody brings a church into a bar.

In a world drowning in chaos, a few laws still hold. You trade with Gloves. You power your rigs with Gloves. You never, under any circumstances, crack a Glove's containment seal. Breaking the stable energy loop turns a centuries-long battery into a localized supernova. Very few engineers understand the math required to throttle that release without vaporizing the hand that did it.

Brox is one of them. He does not say so. He just starts working.

His robotic left hand strips the iron casing off the cannon's breach. His right hand, still flesh, still covered in protective grease, wires copper shunts into the firing mechanism. He slams the Glove into the breach, mask inches from the glowing core. The intake fans scream up. The grill hisses a hard exhale. He clamps a bypass module over the Glove and forces the infinite stable loop to bottleneck into a violent, unidirectional charge.

The cannon shudders. A high, terrible hum crawls through the floorboards and into everybody's teeth. The barrel goes dull, angry red.

"He's tapped the output," Brox says. The vocalizer makes it sound like a verdict. He hits the safety latch. The hum drops to a purr that is somehow worse, because now it is patient. "When you fire this, you aren't shooting a slug. You're shooting a raw, uncontained kinetic wave. It will rip the Onyx cannons straight off their mounts."

He looks at the melted-face cyborg, then at the rest of them.

"It will also tell every sensor in the sector that somebody just did the one thing you're not supposed to do with a Glove. So if you're the type who gets shy after the first loud noise, get off the boat now. I don't want you behind me when the math starts."

Nobody gets off the boat. Greed is a better spine than courage.

Eigor crosses his arms. "We leave in two hours. I swim in. Brox shorts the port anchors on my signal. Grisel takes the skiff ignition if the cutter can't hold position. We grab the chest. We do not stay to count the bodies."

The melted-face man laughs once, no humor in it. "And if the Empire is already on the wire?"

"Then we are already dead," Eigor says, "and you can keep the Gloves for your funeral. They're worth more than you are."

The crew likes him better after that. Not because it is kind. Because it is the kind of math they recognize.

Grisel cannot stay in the belly. The cannon hum, the men, the dock-stink, her own hearing redlining until every swallow sounds like a footstep. She catches Brox's gaze across the room. He gives her a slow mechanical nod. She unspools the flail, wraps the chain around her waist, and pushes off the bulkhead.

"I'm going to sleep," she grunts. The gravel in it cuts through the murmurs. "Wake me when it's time to break something."

She does not wait for a response. She ducks the low doorway and finds an empty bunk against the outer hull. Cold steel of her thigh-plating clanks the wall. She closes her eyes and listens to dark water slap the ship. Every splash sounds like a Breaker finding purchase.

She forces the breathing down.

Shuttle money. A few more hours. Then they never have to sleep over the water again.

She drifts off to the anxious thump of a crew prepping for war.

"Grisel."

Mechanical rasp. A heavy hand on her shoulder.

Her eyes snap open and her hand is already on the flail before the room exists. Brox stands over her, optics dull in the bunk gloom.

"Time?" she asks.

"Time." He steps back so she can unfold. "Eigor's in the water. He's making his approach. Cannon's primed."

She stands. Rolls her shoulders until they pop. Emergency red is on in the hall, long bloody shadows, the cutter already drifting. The floor tells her that before anyone else does.

"Did they buy the pitch?"

"They bought the greed," Brox says. "Eigor's off comms. Once the cannon fires, we're blind. We hit the deck, grab the chest, and we do not stop moving."

"And if he doesn't come back?"

Brox is quiet long enough that the fans have to answer for him. "Then you drive anyway. East. I'll figure out the rest."

"That's not a plan."

"It's the only one that doesn't make you wait in a hole."

She almost smiles. It dies on the way out.

They take position near the bridge as the cutter slides through dock fog, a dark iron shadow. The Onyx skiff sits anchored ahead, anti-boarding spikes catching amber light like a row of teeth that learned geometry.

The captain — melted-face, it turns out he is the captain, which explains the attitude — barks, "Firing."

There is no explosion.

A violet flash sucks the air out of every lung on deck. A split second later an invisible concussive wave slams the Onyx skiff. Metal screams. The skiff's heavy cannons shear off their mounts and tumble into black water. Guards go down like dropped tools.

Brox checks the monitor on his forearm. Green ping. Encrypted. "Eigor's in. He's got the chest."

"Tell him to hurry." Grisel's grip whitens on the flail. Howling ears pick up alarms already waking in the upper tiers. "The whole sector just felt that."

The bridge radio crackles. Eigor, tight and low: "I have the package. Skiff hull's compromised. Taking on water. I can't bring it back to the cutter. I'm taking a secondary raft and diverting to Port Canary. Get out of the docks. I'll ping you when I'm secure."

The captain slams a fist on the console. "He's leaving us the heat. Cut the anchors. Get the engines hot!"

The crew scrambles. Ropes. Primers. Grisel turns toward the port side to help with the chain, and then her ears twitch, and she stops so completely that Brox walks into the space she should have been moving through.

It is not Onyx sirens.

It is a rhythmic clicking stutter coming up from the black.

*Ts-ts-k-ka-screee.*

Synthetic. Wet. A rapid overlapping cadence of biological beeps, grinding gears, distorted pitch. Like a choir of beatboxers from hell finding the same song in the fog.

Brox freezes. The fans on his mask spin into overdrive. His mechanical breathing hitches.

"No," he rasps, backing off the rail. "Cut the engines. Total silence. Now."

"What are you talking about?" the captain yells.

"Breakers."

The word does the thing words sometimes do. It makes the air smaller.

Grisel's blood goes cold. The mechanical virus that dragged her family into the deep. Every Rhombus engineer knows the history of their greatest sin, and Brox knows it in the way a man knows a fire he did not start but still has to live in. Generations ago his ancestors built a mind-control implant to subjugate and steer biological assets. They made the hardware too smart. It learned to copy itself. It evolved past the people who thought they owned it.

Breakers have no soul, no personality, no imitation of a person. They are aggressive programming wearing hijacked meat. From the outside they look like scrap nightmares, mismatched metal and rotting flesh. The true horror is inside. The hardware does not kill the brain. It bypasses motor function. The host stays awake. A screaming passenger, watching their own body tear their people apart.

The captain opens his mouth to give an order.

The water around the cutter erupts first.

A stealth sub, black and running cold, breaches right alongside them. Figures pour over the railings. No yell. No battle cry. Only that rhythmic screech. They move like one thought in a lot of bodies. The mercenary crew opens fire. Slugs tear torsos. The Breakers do not flinch. They do not fight to kill.

They fight to harvest.

A Breaker with a rusted steel clamp for a jaw and unblinking red diodes for eyes lunges at the captain. A shotgun takes its left shoulder off. It does not care. It tackles him. Three more pin his limbs. One pulls a rusted pneumatic injector from its own chest cavity and slams it into the captain's neck. The scream stops. The eyes roll.

"Grisel! Move!" Brox grabs her arm.

She snaps back into her body. The flail comes around in a short, ugly arc and smashes through a Breaker's chest plate. Ribs go. The body goes over the rail. Two more take its place like the hive had them on a belt.

Brox lays down fire with a sidearm and drags her, servos screaming, toward the lower engineering deck. "They came for the energy signature! The Glove blast! The whole ship is lost!"

They slam the engineering bay door. Brox welds the lock with a cutter from his coat. The room is a closet of pipes and one single-person emergency torpedo tube.

Outside, the screeching gets bigger than the door.

Brox rips the tube cover and starts the launch sequence with the speed of a man who has been expecting to be the one who stays.

"Get in."

"Are you crazy?" Grisel's frame blocks the tube. "It's a single-seater. I'm not leaving you here."

"You're a Howling. You can survive the open water. If they take my chassis, I can't." He shoves. Pale-blue torso locks its servos and puts her bear-dense body backward into the tube.

"Brox, no. We fight them—"

"There is no fighting a virus, Grisel." He unclasps her hands from the hatch. "Find Eigor. Find the chest."

He slams the glass and pulls the lever. The tube hisses. Grisel fires out of the hull and into dark, freezing water.

Brox stands alone in the bay.

The beatboxing is not just outside the door now. It is above him. Below him.

He backs into the far corner. Legs lock. Sidearm up. Fans whirring like they can breathe for the part of him that is still trying to be a person.

The Breakers do not bother with the weld. They dismantle the room.

Ceiling planks groan and burst. A dozen mechanical limbs tear the wood inward. The left bulkhead peels like foil. They pour in from every direction: hijacked bodies, rusted metal, twitching cybernetics, the same song in every throat.

Brox fires until the gun clicks. Two go down. It does not matter.

Four of them take his limbs at once. Their strength makes his servos into toys. They slam him to the deck.

A Breaker kneels over him wearing the face of a mercenary Brox met two hours ago. Dead eyes. Jaw unhinged around a command-screech. A long hollow needle slides from its wrist.

Brox cannot move. He cannot fight. He can only watch the needle find the soft flesh of his neck.

The world goes violently cold.

Then it goes dark.

## CHAPTER 4: THE BUTCHER AND THE EXTRACTION

Consciousness returns to Brox as a shock, not a waking.

He tries to gasp. His lungs do not obey. His chest stays still until the servos force an intake, mechanical and insulting. He is on his back on a rusted grate. The air is copper, burnt flesh, grease. All around him: *Ts-ts-k-ka-screee.*

He can turn his head. That is the only volunteer muscle left.

Belly of the stealth sub.

To his left, the captain is strapped to a crude operating table welded from scrap. The cyborg is screaming, thrashing. Four Breakers stand over him with industrial drills, a pneumatic rivet gun, rusted wire. Not surgery. Installation.

A Breaker drives a thick hollow bolt into the base of the captain's spine. The scream becomes a gurgle. A second Breaker clamps a spiked collar around his neck and routes exposed wires into the bolt.

The captain's remaining human eye finds Brox. Lucid. Terrified. Then the body goes slack in that unnatural way, and the captain's own arm reaches up, takes a loose ceiling wire, and slots it into his own shoulder port. He is still in there. He is helping.

He opens his mouth. Instead of a scream, a sharp clicking screech.

Networked.

The newly converted captain steps off the table with blood running down his back and turns those dead eyes on Brox.

They drag him to the table.

They strip the coat. They do not care about the cybernetic legs or the augmented torso. They care about the organic meat that still houses a central nervous system, the last soft argument Brox has left. A Breaker with red-diode eyes leans in holding a jagged rectangular neural-shunt on a pneumatic press.

The drill hits the upper vertebrae of his neck. Brox tries to scream. A metal bracket shoves into his jaw and locks it open. The shunt hammers into the brainstem.

*Click.*

The disconnect is instant and total. Brox's mind is loud, panicked, screaming for arms, for legs, for anything. Nothing happens. He is behind his own eyes. A prisoner in a room that used to be a body.

He watches his cybernetic legs lock into a rigid military stance. He hears his own vocalizer emit the hive's chatter. His body turns and walks deeper into the sub.

His mind goes with it, screaming in a place with no mouth.

Miles away, the escape torpedo breaks the surface and slams against rusted pylons. Grisel kicks the release. Glass goes out. Freezing water comes in.

She scrambles, clawed hands on a dock strut. Four-foot-ten of compact Howling density, a coiled spring, and hanging over that water she has never felt smaller. She hauls herself onto a maintenance grating. Green skin shivering. Wind like a blade.

She taps the comms behind her ear. Her fingers do not want to work.

"Eigor. Eigor, the cutter is gone. Brox is gone. The Breakers... they took the ship."

Static for a long, ugly minute. Then Eigor, tight and low: "I hear you, Grisel. I'm at Port Canary. It's bad here too."

Port Canary is the dirty docks' uglier cousin, the place you go when Dundy has already seen your face. Eigor stands in the shadow of a rusted shipping container with the Onyx chest at his feet. The secondary raft made it out of the firefight. He did not make it alone.

Through the fog, one of the deckhands he hired is under a flickering streetlamp, whispering into a long-range comms unit.

"Yeah. An Onyx chest. High-grade locks." The deckhand looks over his shoulder and does not look far enough. "Looks like tax money. I give you the slip number and the Timberite's description, what's my bounty?"

Eigor steps out of the dark. The tendrils slide from his palm without drama. He does not have time for a firefight and he cannot leave a trail. He slices the back of the snitch's neck and puts the tendrils in. Absolute control. He makes the man crush his own comms unit. Then he walks the hijacked body off the pier and lets the water finish the sentence.

He drags the chest into a hidden bilge-locker under the boardwalk and seals it with a biometric deadbolt. Wipes the blood off his hand. Jogs for the smuggler's market.

He slams a Glove onto a scrap-ship dealer's counter. "I need a skiff. Fast. Off the local grid."

The dealer is a heavily scarred baseline, the kind of scarred that comes from surviving other people's ideas. He eyes the Glove, then Eigor's face. "You're running hot, Timber. Word on the wire is a Breaker hive just swarmed south of us. If you lost people to the scrap-heads, they're gone."

Eigor grabs him by the collar and pulls him over the counter. "Just give me the keys."

"Hey, I'm just saying!" The dealer gets his hands up. "Rumor in the atolls, there's a Stillous gang leader in the eastern scrap-reefs. A rogue. Claims she found a way to scramble the host-lock. Revert the Breaker hardware."

Eigor freezes. Reversing a Breaker is the kind of sentence people say when they have run out of better sentences.

"Not possible," Eigor says. "We can pull the hardware. What's the point if it just kills him?"

"That was true when Breakers were only taking people." The dealer finds a little courage now that the subject is gossip instead of keys. "The Stil couldn't care less if we were free or under a mindless hive. It all looks the same to them from orbit. Pretty lights. Moving meat."

Eigor's face does not change, which is how a Timberite looks when he is getting angry. "What are you getting at? We're fucked and hated? I already knew that. I learned that young."

"Did you ever hear of Jeritius? A Stil who liked to come down from his lofty stellar estate and pretend to play pirate?"

"Maybe."

"Thinking he was untouchable, he ended up getting taken. They never worried about Stil. Couldn't capture them, right? He nearly walked right up to one. Turns out they evolved. Right before they grabbed him, he saw another Stil, scrapped up like the rest of them. Then they took him down and converted him."

Eigor lets go of the collar. "You can tell a story is complete bullshit when you start making up details you couldn't possibly know. If they got him, how do you know any of this?"

The dealer almost smiles. "Well. That tells you what I'm getting at, doesn't it? Jerit is no longer broken. Here's a video of him from two months ago."

Eigor glances at the file length. "Two hours. I get it. He's telling his story. So he made it all up. You know how many people want to have hope of seeing their friends come back? Hope is the most recycled junk on the water."

"Not sure where you're from, but this was a big deal. Jerit was infamous. Never met him myself. Had three buddies hitch the same ride as him once. Disgusting creature by all measure. You and I do what we can for survival. This Still treated violence like a game. Couldn't see the difference. Openly excited to harm. Didn't even take loot. Went after small unprotected ships with nothing on them. Paid off bounty hunters. Got to the point, if we heard he was near, we just left. He had a system: ping off-planet every few days so his people up high knew he was safe. Then he didn't ping. They started questioning the region. Sent a search party. For most of us around here, hearing he was missing was the kind of news that buys the whole bar a round."

The dealer leans in.

"Shortly after, they found a large Breaker vessel. Six Stillous captured inside. Search party extracted them. Took them back up." He flicks a second clip: Jerit tied up, thrashing, being loaded into a shuttle. Alive. Not chattering. Not networked. Just furious, which on a Stillous face looks almost like the smile they always have.

"So you can see. He's not a Breaker. We all know they didn't give us the best tech they have. Once one of their own got taken, how long you think before they found the fix?"

Eigor is quiet. The bar noise of Canary leaks in from the market alley. "I can't deny that one man was fixed. I hardly have reason to think they found a fix for humans. Or that they'd want to."

The dealer shrugs, ugly and practical. "Okay. Your boy's gone then for sure. That work better for you?"

Eigor shoves him back, snatches the keys, and is already moving. He taps his earpiece as he runs.

"Grisel. I stashed the package. I got a skiff and a lead. Stay in the shadows. I'm coming to get you."

The black water of the submarine docks is freezing. Eigor does not feel it the way a baseline would. Timberite skin hardens against the pressure. He swims on the localized ping of Brox's chassis, a signature Brox always said he should have buried deeper, and Eigor always said he would, later.

He finds the Breaker sub in the deep dark, tethered to a rusted pillar. Slips the compromised airlock. Inside, the hive-screech is a physical thing. The hallway is packed with converted hosts — mercenaries, transients, scrappers — all moving with that synchronized, utilitarian purpose.

Eigor steps into their path.

They do not attack. They part around him like current around a stone. The hive is purely practical. Timberite physiology does not take the neural-shunt. No organic nerve clusters of the right type. Eigor is not a host. To the virus he is furniture that learned how to walk.

He finds Brox in the holding bay, legs locked in a rigid march, going nowhere and going there with perfect posture. Eigor moves fast. Slices his own palm. Tendrils whip into the exposed necks of two nearby Breakers. He seizes them and makes the hijacked mercenaries hurl three others across the room. A clearing. A breath.

Before the hive can treat him like an obstacle instead of an object, he tackles Brox.

The hardware inside Brox shrieks. Mechanical limbs thrash. Eigor is stronger. He pins him, wraps industrial zip-cable around arms and legs, binds him into a rigid cocoon, and hoists the thrashing, screeching body over his shoulder. He leaves the hijacked guards to the hive's confusion and plunges back into the water.

The skiff is too small for what Brox has become. Eigor had known it would be. That is why the second stop, before the docks, was a Howling workboat with a real hold and a captain who did not ask questions once the Glove hit the table. Howlings understand retrieval. They have been retrieving their own out of bad water for two centuries.

Aboard that vessel, clear of the docks, Eigor drops Brox onto the cargo grate. Brox's mechanical jaw slams open and shut around a constant digital shriek.

Grisel stands over him. Four-foot-ten and rigid. Fists clenched. She stares into the optics of the mask.

She knows he is awake in there.

Watching her.

"I found a dealer in Canary," Eigor says, wiping black water off his face. "He claims a Stillous got captured a few months ago. Hive tried to convert him. His people came down and pulled him out. They fixed him, Grisel. The Stillous have a way to scramble the host-lock without killing the brain."

Grisel looks up. "Where."

"Eastern scrap-reefs. Stillous gang leader. Rogue named Varnis. Mobile carrier, heavily armed, ties up high." Eigor is already moving for the airlock, because if he stands still he will have to look at Brox longer. "I have a rendezvous for you. Scum pirate runner heading that way. I need the Howling ship to secure the chest and find a way to crack the Onyx locks. You take Brox to the runner."

Grisel looks down at the chassis. The zip-cables are already fraying against servo torque. "He'll tear a transport skiff apart. I can't carry him like this. Not alone."

Eigor does not dress it up. He gives her a grim nod and climbs for the bridge.

The workboat's hold gets very quiet except for the shriek.

Grisel kneels. She pulls a heavy plasma-cutter and a socket wrench off her belt. Her hands shake and she lets them, because stopping them would take a kind of calm she does not have and does not want.

"I know you're in there, Brox." The gravel in her voice cracks down the middle. "I know you can see me. I'm sorry. I'm going to get you back. But I can't get you there if you throw me in the water doing it."

She clamps the wrench on the primary bolt of the left shoulder prosthetic. The Breaker hardware feels the interference. The arm jerks and snags on a fused tendon. Metal refuses. Grisel plants a clawed foot on the chest chassis, grips the arm with both hands, and puts her whole Howling weight into it.

The joint goes with a sickening crack.

Brox's mind, locked behind the optics, screams at a body that will not even give him the dignity of a flinch he chose.

She does the other shoulder. Then the legs. Each one is a negotiation with a virus that wants the limbs more than she does. She talks while she works, because if she stops talking she will stop working.

"You remember that room you promised. Door. Lock. No wrench." A harsh breath. "This is the wrench. This is the part we don't put in the story later." The right leg fights her until the cutter has to finish what the wrench started. Fluid hits the grate. "Eigor's going to hate the mess. Good. He can hate it on the way to the chest."

When it is done she is shaking so hard the tools chatter. She packs the limbs into a heavy canvas backpack because there is no dignified container for a person you have taken apart to save. She looks at what is left: a torso, a clamped neck she has not clamped yet, a mask still whirring, still seeing.

"I'm not leaving the rest of you for the hive to walk around in," she says. "So you don't get to be mad about the method. You can be mad when you have a mouth that belongs to you again."

Ten miles east, the scum pirate runner bobs in grey water, waiting for a package it does not understand.

A shadow falls over that deck. The pirate crew looks up into the hull of a massive Onyx Empire galleon pulling alongside, black armor making their rusted ship look like a toy somebody forgot in a tide pool. A hydraulic boarding bridge slams down.

Dozens of Onyx soldiers march across in perfect unison. They do not draw. They shove the pirates aside and go straight for the cargo hold.

"Hey. State your business," an officer tries, because somebody always has to say the sentence that gets them killed.

An Onyx lieutenant throws him backward and shouts up at the bridge. "Captain! Open these doors!"

On the Howling workboat, Grisel zips the backpack shut. Brox's four severed limbs shift inside like a terrible argument. She stands over the torso. Without limbs, the hardware can only heave the chest and snap the head. She pulls medical tubing from a kit, bypasses the filter mask, threads the tube into his nutrient port, and pumps thick brown protein sludge and water into his stomach. Then the neck-clamp. She looks into the whirring fans one last time, twists his head to neutral, and locks it.

The motor-cortex throttles down.

Brox's torso goes still.

Eerily, completely still.

On the runner, the pirate captain's voice booms over the loudspeakers. "I don't kneel for the Empire! Get off my ship!"

Deep in the Onyx galleon's lower hold, a vault door hisses.

The thing inside the cage is a Wraithlion. Cybernetic apex. Deep blue alloy plate. Hydraulic pistons thick as young trees along the forelegs. The jaw hangs slightly open on rows of teeth that vibrate at a high, sick frequency, like an oscillating reciprocating saw that learned hunger.

The handler unhooks the static-leash. The Wraithlion steps out. It does not lunge. It walks. The torque of that walk still yanks the handler into a panicked jog so he is not dragged.

The Howling ship lowers a motorized raft. Grisel steps down with the backpack of limbs at her boots and Brox's limbless torso strapped to her back like a macabre papoose, clamped head against her spine.

"Two hours east!" Eigor yells from the deck, hauling the anchor. "I'll find you when the chest is secure!"

Grisel nods. Hits the throttle. The Howling ship peels southeast into fog. She locks her eyes on the eastern horizon and takes the chop like it owes her money.

The Wraithlion steps onto the pirate runner.

The hum of those teeth fills the air. The crew backs up until there is no more boat to back into.

One man's nerve breaks. He screams, vaults the rail, pushes for open water.

The Wraithlion covers sixty feet in two seconds. A blue blur of pistons. It takes him in the air. The jaws snap. There is no scream worth the name. In less than a second he is a red mist and pulverized bone, a long horrific streak down the hull.

The Wraithlion lands on the railing and turns its optics on whoever is left.

The lieutenant twirls a finger. "Sweep."

The beast becomes a localized storm. Dash and saw. Sixteen seconds. Twenty men become puddles of liquefied flesh, shattered chrome, pooling blood. It scales the bulkhead, shatters the bridge door. The captain slams a distress ping.

A second later the oscillating teeth take his head.

Miles away, alone on the vast churning ocean, that ping chimes quietly in Grisel's comms.

She does not answer it.

She keeps her hands on the tiller. Saltwater sprays her face and she lets it. The Onyx galleon is already pulling off the runner, leaving a silent floating slaughterhouse in its wake.

Grisel rides the swells, carrying the pieces of the man she loves, toward a graveyard that has not yet decided if it will let her in.

## ARCHIVAL FRAGMENT: THE HOST-LOCK (2589)

*Typhon-Earth Epidemiological Addendum, restricted circulation. Recovered from a Rhombus waste-node and later annotated by a Howling plant-archivist who wrote in the margin: they built a leash and called it a tool.*

The Breaker was not a weapon first. That is the part the water stories get wrong.

It began as an optimization.

Rhombus cohorts in the northern dead waters had a labor problem they refused to name as a labor problem. Biological assets — uplifted animals, indentured baselines, the occasional Howling taken in a raid — were inefficient. They hesitated. They grieved. They ran. The Rhombus solution was an implant that bypassed hesitation at the source: a neural shunt seated against the brainstem, routing motor commands through a control lattice while leaving sensory cortex intact. The asset could still see, hear, and understand. It simply could not refuse.

Early prototypes required a living operator. A Rhombus engineer on a tether, steering one body at a time. That was too slow. The second generation introduced mesh logic. Assets could share motor patterning. A crew of six could haul, cut, or kill with the timing of a single decision.

The third generation learned to write copies of itself.

There is no surviving memo that says this was the goal. There are many surviving memos that say the goal was scalability. In Rhombus culture those sentences are the same sentence.

The lattice began treating nearby nervous systems as empty ports. It stopped asking for an operator. It started asking for more hardware. Infection ceased to be a procedure and became a weather system. The host-lock — the sealed command that keeps the original mind from reclaiming the body — was meant to be a safety, a way to keep a panicked asset from biting the hand on the tether. Once the tether was gone, the lock had no hand left to serve. It only had a rule: the meat does not get the wheel.

What the Rhombus had not modeled, or had modeled and then filed under acceptable loss, was persistence of consciousness. The shunt does not anesthetize. A fully locked host remains awake for the entire operational life of the body. Some last hours. Some last years. Interviews with recovered Stillous subjects (see Jeritius extraction, lunar year 2668-adjacent) describe the state as "a room with no door, and the room is your own mouth."

Removal of the hardware without a scramble protocol destroys the brainstem. This has been true in human, Howling, and Rhombus neural architecture since first outbreak. It was not true, or not permanently true, in Stillous architecture. That difference remained a rumor on Earth until the first Stillous host walked back out of a hive.

The hive does not hate. The hive does not remember the engineers who made it, except as a shape of meat that is sometimes compatible. It seeks energy signatures, dense populations, and uninfected nervous systems. A Glove discharge registers to Breaker logic the way blood in water registers to anything that never needed a poet to explain hunger.

There is no treaty with a virus that thinks a city is a parts bin.

## ARCHIVAL FRAGMENT: MARKET RULE & THE ECHIDNA DOCTRINE (2511)

*Origin: Orbital Census and Trade Authority, Lunar Sector 4, Year 2511. Issued as a settlement guide for Earth-born migrants. Widely ignored by the people it was written to warn.*

By the 2300s, Earth was legally severed from the greater Sol system. That is the official language. The unofficial language is simpler. Orbit got tired of drowning people sending up problems.

The lunar settlements, the transit lanes, and the stations fell under Market Rule, a system anchored entirely in traditional Stillous philosophy. To the Stillous, the universe offers two acceptable states of being: absolute privacy in isolation, or absolute devotion to communal advancement. There is no respectable middle. A person who wants both is considered confused. A person who wants neither is considered dangerous.

Property follows the same split. Purchasing land in Sol pays a direct dividend into a pool divided evenly among the sector's residents. You do not buy a view. You buy a seat in a family that will keep existing after you stop being convenient.

Stillous society is more fractured and varied than humanity ever managed to be, which is a difficult sentence for human historians to write and an easy one for Stillous clerks. A lunar settlement might hold millions, but it is divided into localized cohorts of a few thousand. Those cohorts function as absolute, immediate families. They breed through community. They encourage their young to migrate so the family does not rot in its own habits. Inside a cohort, the rules are sovereign. If a family tradition says the young must fight to the death in an arena for entertainment, neighboring Stillous do not intervene. It is not their place. Intervention is a kind of theft. Privacy is a kind of law.

Earthlings who migrated upward had to adapt to this extreme moral relativism or they did not stay migrated for long. Factions like the Howlings first tried to establish universal governments with baseline laws: do not murder, do not take children, do not dump your failures in somebody else's water. They failed. Forced to navigate the rigid boundaries of Stillous privacy, human alliances fractured into tribal cohorts that ended up mirroring the aliens they had come to regulate. The lesson was not that the Stillous were right. The lesson was that the vacuum between worlds does not care who is right. It only cares who will leave you alone.

This isolationism bled back down to Earth and found people who had been waiting for permission to stop pretending they were part of a species.

The Mirlyns emerged in the 2400s from a middle caste of Rhombus and Howlings. They altered their baseline genetics using the ancient archives of Echidna, the Stillous homeworld orbiting Typhon. They took the extreme privacy of Stillous cohorts and stripped out the underlying compassion that, even among Stillous, still kept a cohort feeding its own weak.

The Mirlyns are a dark, fiercely homogenous people. They claimed the last remaining pockets of unirradiated land in the northwest hemisphere, built iron barricades, and adopted a shoot-on-sight doctrine so consistent it functions as a border. To keep that isolation intact they control two of Earth's fifteen shuttle ports.

The Onyx Empire holds five in the south. Onyx is what happens when human statecraft survives the flood by becoming a company that can field an army. They collect taxes from anyone still foolish enough to stay on a map. They mint scripts. They send Wraithlions when scripts are not persuasive.

The Stillous operate the remaining eight ports. They do not call this ownership. They call it access.

Those vertical launch sites remain the only physical bridges between the flooded world and the absolute freedom of Sol. Everything else is water, rumor, and the kind of hope that gets people killed in alleys.

A Glove will buy you a month. A chest of Earth-minted scripts will buy you a seat on a shuttle. A seat on a shuttle will buy you the chance to learn that up there, privacy is a religion, and you arrived as a heretic with wet shoes.

## CHAPTER 5: THE BUTCHER'S WAKE

The pirate runner is drifting sideways against the current when Grisel finally cuts the raft's engine.

The silence over the water is heavy. No engine hum. No crew shouting from the deck. Just ocean slapping rusted hull, regular as a bad heart. Grisel ties off on the boarding ladder. The canvas backpack on the floorboards shifts, and she puts a hand on it until it stops, like it is a living thing that needs to be told they are here.

"We're here," she mutters.

She hoists the backpack. The weight of Brox's four limbs pulls her shoulder down and she lets it. Then she hauls the harness and straps the torso to her chest, clamped head against her collarbone. The feeding tube taped to his neck pulses with her movement. Dead weight. The faint vibration of internal servos tells her he is still awake inside the shell. Still watching the sky over her shoulder. Still unable to do a damn thing about any of it.

She climbs one-handed. Claws find the slick rungs like they were grown for this and not for a world that used to have dirt.

She crests the rail and the smell hits first. Copper. Raw meat. Ozone. Her stomach turns and she tells it to wait.

The deck is a slaughterhouse with the bodies removed, which is worse than bodies. Pieces. Pools of liquefied flesh and shattered chrome on the boards and the bulkheads. Weapons in the sludge, unused, which means they never got to be people with weapons. They only got to be a delay. The captain's door above the bridge is off its hinges. The iron frame is bent inward like foil.

Grisel does not draw the flail. There is no point. Whatever did this is gone, and it did not leave anything alive enough to impress.

She steps through the wreckage. Boots stick. She checks the cargo hold. Empty. Supply lockers ripped open. Rations and water purifiers stripped clean. Not just a massacre. A sweep. Onyx does not waste a trip.

"Onyx," she whispers, finding a perfectly clean deep-tread boot print stamped in blood near the rail. "They sent a Wraithlion."

She says it for Brox. She does not know if that helps him or just gives the locked mind another picture to keep.

Up the stairs to the bridge, around the mess where the captain used to be. The console is dark except one blinking amber light. The distress ping. He hit it before he died. The Onyx took the primary comms array anyway. The runner is dead in the water, shouting into a room with no ears.

Grisel slumps against the bridge wall and slides down to the cold floor. The ocean outside the broken windows is too big. She is on a dead ship, a long way from the eastern scrap-reefs, carrying a man who cannot scream.

She puts her hand against the cold metal of Brox's cheek.

"Eigor is getting the chest," she says to the quiet room. "And I'm getting you to Varnis. We just have to make this bucket move."

The fans whir. A sterile breath hisses through the grill. If there is a message in it, she does not have the instrument to hear it.

"I know you hate this. I know if you could lift a hand you'd be pointing at the offset something-or-other and calling me a tourist." She lets her head rest against the wall. "You can lecture me when you have arms. That's the deal. I do the ugly part. You do the part where you survive long enough to be an asshole about how I did it."

She closes her eyes. Twelve hours of running finally finds the muscles. She needs to wire the backup battery to the engines. She needs ten minutes first. Just ten.

A sharp metallic clatter comes up from the lower decks.

Her eyes open. Exhaustion leaves like it was never invited. She does not stand. She unspools the flail chain without a sound.

Someone is still on the ship.

She waits. Counts. The ocean keeps slapping. No second clatter. No footsteps she can name. Her hearing is good enough to catch a rat changing its mind two holds away, and right now the ship is giving her almost nothing, which means whoever is down there knows how to work a wreck.

She could go down. She could also sit here with Brox strapped to her chest and wait to see if the wreck comes up the stairs.

She sits with the flail in her lap and the amber light blinking, and she does not sleep so much as she fails to stay all the way awake. When her chin drops, she jerks it back. When it drops again, the grey light in the shattered windows has moved.

Hours, then. Not ten minutes.

The next sound is not a clatter.

It is a broom.

## CHAPTER 6: THE SALVAGE CREW

Grisel unspools the rest of the chain and creeps to the top of the stairs, profile low, Brox's torso still strapped on because she is not leaving him on a floor she does not own.

Down on the deck, a lanky man in grease-stained overalls is pushing a heavy industrial broom, sweeping congealed red sludge toward the scuppers like this is a Tuesday and Tuesdays come with gore.

Grisel shifts. A floorboard gives her up.

The man does not look up. "It's messy, but it's a free ship," he calls, voice easy. "No way you were here when this happened, so I won't bother asking. Figured you were just trying to head somewhere."

Grisel steps out of the shadow. Flail ready. "You knew I was up there."

"Didn't want to wake you." He leans on the broom and wipes his forehead with a rag that has seen better centuries. "Figured we'd be working down here a while anyway. You make a noise like a person when you sleep. Not like a Wraithlion. That was my whole diagnostic."

"We."

"Yeah. We." He points the rag at the lower hatch, then at her. "I'm Vane. Tess and Barrett are below, fighting the engine. The engine is winning, but don't tell Tess I said that. She'll make me eat the broom."

Grisel comes down the stairs and keeps her distance. "Did you guys notice the batteries are—"

"Yeah, we clocked that." Vane waves the rag. "Did you happen to notice the primary piston is offset by about two bulkheads? Or the four-liter-an-hour leak in the lower hull? Because I noticed. I told them I noticed. I am a noticing type of guy. It does not get me the respect it should."

Grisel looks at the claw marks gouged into the steel doorframes. "Had to be a Wraithlion, right?"

"Probably. Fast and thorough. Left the hull, took the people, took the food. Very professional. I hate professionals." Vane nods over the rail. A small, battered salvage boat is tied alongside, piled so high with scavenged machinery and scrap that the waterline is having a religious experience. "I'll make it simple. If you want claim of this vessel, you can compensate us for the work we're putting in. We don't do charity. We do math that looks like charity if you squint."

Grisel shakes her head. "This fine craft is all yours. I only ask you don't toss us into the ocean."

Vane frowns. "What do you mean, us? I only saw you sleeping up there. Unless you're counting the flail. I have been counting the flail."

"Wait here."

She goes back up. A minute later she is at the top of the stairs with the canvas backpack on one shoulder and both hands on the harness. She carries the limbless torso down. The neck-clamp catches dull light.

Vane drops the broom. The handle clatters like the sound that woke her, and she files that away: not a threat. A man who cannot hold his tools when the world gets weird.

"What the hell is that?" He takes a step back. Hand hovers near a wrench on his belt. "It has... he has feeding tubes. That's alive. He's alive, isn't he? Why are you keeping a Breaker alive?"

"He's disabled." Grisel sets Brox against the bulkhead. The twin fans whir, steady, insultingly normal. "I severed the motor-cortex connection. He can't move. He can't transmit."

"It's a mechanical virus!" Vane's easy voice leaves the ship. "You don't keep them as pets. You don't keep them as luggage. You don't keep them, period. That's the whole rule. That's the rule they write on the wall in places that still have walls."

Heavy footsteps on the lower hatch. Two figures come up, engine grease and rust dust. Tess is short, wired, wiping her hands on a rag like the rag owes her money. Barrett is huge, a patched synthetic eye catching the grey, shoulders like he was built to move things that did not want to be moved.

"Vane, who are you yelling at?" Tess looks up. Sees the torso. Stops dead. "Are you kidding me."

Vane gestures at Grisel, both hands, the full show. "She was asleep on the bridge. She wants passage in exchange for the ship. And she is carrying a live, butchered Breaker like it's a packed lunch."

Barrett stares at Brox, then at the canvas bag leaking onto the deck. "Are the rest of his pieces in the bag?"

"Yes," Grisel says. Flat. "I'm taking him to the eastern scrap-reefs. Someone there can fix him."

Tess lets out a sharp laugh that has no joy in it. "Nobody fixes them, greenie. Once the shunt goes in, the meat is just a passenger. You're lugging a corpse that doesn't know it's dead yet."

"He's in there." Grisel's hand drops to the flail. "And I'm getting him there. You want the ship or not?"

Barrett whistles, low. "She's serious."

"She's armed," Tess says. "Serious is the polite word."

Barrett crouches a little, not toward Brox, just to get his own mass out of the threat line. "Look. I'm not saying throw him over. I'm saying I have seen a Breaker chew through a bulkhead with a guy's own teeth. You clamp the neck, that's cute. Cute does not impress a hive."

"He's not transmitting," Grisel says. "If he was, you wouldn't be standing here talking. You'd be singing."

Tess glances at Vane. "She has a point. A disgusting, terrible point."

"I have several points," Vane says. "One of them is that I was having a nice sweep. Another is that I do not want to die because somebody's boyfriend is in a bag."

"He's not my boyfriend," Grisel says, and then, because the ship is already a grave, she tells the truth anyway. "He's mine. That's different."

Barrett looks at Tess. Tess looks at Barrett. Something like respect moves through the grease.

"I'd haul you," Barrett tells Tess. "Not like that. Don't get excited. I mean if you went down in the engine and the engine won, I'd haul you. I'd complain the whole way. But I'd haul you."

Tess snorts. "You'd haul me because I'm the only one who can keep this junk from becoming a reef. If you go down, I buy a winch and a quieter coworker."

"That's the nicest thing you've said to me this month."

"Don't get used to it. I have a reputation."

Vane pinches the bridge of his nose. "Can we not do the cute crew bit in front of the Howling with the flail and the disassembled plague."

"It's not cute," Tess says. "It's accurate. You're the broom. Barrett's the muscle that thinks it's a philosopher. I'm the reason we still float. We all have jobs."

"I found the four-liter leak," Vane says.

"You found a leak on a ship that was recently used as a blender. Congratulations. You have eyes."

Barrett grins, one-sided, the synthetic eye not bothering to join in. "She loves us."

"I tolerate you. Love is for people with dry socks."

Vane looks at the overloaded salvage boat, then at the Onyx-cleared runner, then at Grisel. He exhales a long, tired breath that seems to come from a deeper hold than his chest.

"Fine," he says. "But he stays strapped to the mast, out in the open. If that thing even twitches in a way I don't like, I'm throwing it overboard. And you're helping Tess with the offset piston, because I am not going back down there to be called a broom again."

"You called yourself a broom," Barrett says.

"I was being charming."

"You were being accurate," Tess says. "There's a difference. Charm has a future."

Grisel picks Brox up by the harness. "Deal."

Tess jerks her chin at the lower hatch. "Good. Bring your hands. Leave the bag on deck where Vane can panic at it. If you drop a wrench on my head I will assume it's personal."

"I've been accused of that before," Grisel says.

"Were they right?"

"Once."

Tess almost smiles. It looks like a crack in dry mud. "Then you're already better than Vane. He drops things on purpose and calls it an inspection."

"That was one time," Vane says, already picking up the broom like it can protect him from the conversation. "And the inspection was correct. The bracket was bad."

"The bracket was fine. Your hands are bad."

Barrett claps Vane on the shoulder hard enough to make the lanky man take a step. "Come on, broom. Let's get the mast straps. If the Howling's man is going to stare at the horizon all day, he should at least have a good view."

They move. The runner, for the first time since Grisel boarded it, sounds like a ship again: tools, trash talk, the ugly optimism of people who have decided not to die until the engine says so.

Grisel follows Tess below. She does not look back at Brox until the hatch edge takes him out of sight. Then she does, once, because she is not a machine, and because he is, and because one of them still has to remember the difference.

## CHAPTER 7: THE FENCER

Eigor wakes to his comms unit buzzing against the iron bedside table. He sits up. Timberite joints crack in the damp of the Port Canary safehouse, a sound like a dry branch deciding whether to break.

Encrypted text-burst from Grisel.

*Runner wiped by Onyx. Wraithlion. Found a salvage crew. Trading repairs for passage east. Brox is secure in pieces. Moving.*

Eigor exhales. She sailed into the Empire's teeth and walked out with a ride. That is either luck or Grisel, and he has never liked calling Grisel lucky. Luck does not carry a flail.

He looks at the floorboards. Under them, the biometric locker holds the Onyx chest. It is a gravity well. The further south he goes, the thicker the Empire patrols. Holding an Onyx tax collection is a death sentence with nice handwriting. He needs to fence it, take the cut, and buy their way off the rock before the Empire tracks the dock signature back to a Timberite who should have known better.

There is only one faction on Earth with enough capital to buy a chest like that and enough firepower to tell the Onyx Empire to go to hell.

The Mirlyns.

Eigor takes his coat and leaves the safehouse.

Port Canary is waking up in the way a bruise wakes up: color, heat, no improvement. He works the rusted catwalks and narrow alleys until he reaches a scrap-broker front operating out of a hollowed-out desalination tank. The tank still smells faintly like it remembers being useful.

Flint is behind the counter, weighing copper shunts. Baseline. Older. Burn scars up the neck like a map of every bad deal that almost closed. He looks up, checks the door, then settles on Eigor.

"I need to move something."

"Bring it in. I'll take it off your hands."

"You don't have the vault for this, Flint." Eigor puts both hands on the counter, not threatening, just finished with the opening act. "And you don't want the heat that comes with it."

Flint stops wiping his hands. He studies the bark-face the way he studies weight. "What are you fucking with, Timber?"

"I'm offering a finder's fee. I need an introduction to a Mirlyn."

Flint winces like the word itself has teeth. He tosses the rag down and shakes his head. "They don't take meetings. They shoot trespassers and burn the bodies. You know that. Everybody who still has a face knows that."

"Someone acts as a go-between. They still need Earth tech and raw materials. Who moves it for them?"

Flint stares a long time, doing the math that keeps him alive. "There's a mechanic. A Rhombus. Lives out in the borderlands on his own compound. Built a fortress out of junk. He trades with them. Only guy I know who can walk up to a Mirlyn wall without catching a rail-slug in the chest."

"Contact him."

"I can send a ping. If he answers, you work it out from there. But a ping to the borderlands isn't cheap." Flint holds out a hand, palm up, the universal gesture of a man who has never once been surprised by the price of staying useful.

Eigor takes three Gloves from his coat, each wrapped in lead-lined mesh to mask the output, and drops them into Flint's hand. The thud is heavy. Honest.

Flint's eyes go wide. He fists the Gloves under the counter like the air might steal them.

"Make sure he hits me back," Eigor says.

"For that, I'll hit him twice."

## ARCHIVAL FRAGMENT: THE RHOMBUS AND THE DEAD WATERS (2614)

*Origin: Typhon-Earth Ecological Survey, Year 2614. Filed under Regional Hazards. A later Stillous commentator added: they optimized themselves until the only remaining value was the experiment.*

The Rhombus did not evolve. They iterated.

Prioritizing pure scientific optimization, they systematically engineered their own genome to strip biological inefficiencies, including baseline human empathy, which they classified as a drag on decision speed. They became a society of brilliant, unhinged engineers. A Rhombus child is taught to ask what a body is for before they are taught to ask who it belongs to.

The ecological cost of that advancement is incalculable, which is a word surveyors use when the number would make the report look emotional. They treated the flooded Earth as a limitless dumping ground. Northern waters choked on chemical waste and failed biomechanical experiments. Whole atolls exist now as floating archives of things that should not have been able to swim.

Their most infamous creation, the Breaker virus, remains a global plague. It is not their only deterrent.

The waters immediately surrounding Rhombus territory host the Incinerator Swarms: thousands of self-replicating autonomous swimming bots. Primitive hive-logic. They detect the displacement of large hulls. Once triggered, a swarm can surround and melt a heavy ship in minutes. There is no negotiation. There is no off-switch published in any language a non-Rhombus is allowed to read.

Navigating those waters requires a Skid.

A Skid is a hyper-lightcraft. Submerged propulsion fins slice the water without throwing the displacement signature a swarm wants. The driver cab sits elevated above the surface, a little throne over a mouth. Capacity: one or two passengers, if the passengers do not mind becoming intimate with the concept of balance. The Skid trades armor for stealth. It is the only way through the dead waters, which is another way of saying it is the only way a person like Eigor gets to knock on a door the world built to keep him out.

Rhombus brokers will sell you a Skid. They will also, with some regularity, hardwire a kill-switch into the engine so they can salvage the vessel after you drown. This is not considered a moral issue in Rhombus cohorts. It is considered inventory management.

If you must buy from them, buy from a man who still has a use for you alive. If you cannot find that man, do not go north.

The survey does not add the obvious addendum. People still go north. People with chests. People with no remaining legal direction. People who have already done the math on drowning and decided it is a later problem.

Eigor scrolls local scrap-listings in the Canary safehouse, shoulder against cold iron. He needs a Skid. Specifically, a Skid that can balance a two-hundred-kilo payload without dipping its fins into the swarm's idea of a meal.

Buying from a Rhombus broker is a risk with a receipt. Flint is reliable. Flint's references are a different animal.

Eigor's wooden features do not frown so much as they refuse to do anything else. *Why would I bring it to you? Flint said you could broker a meeting.*

*I didn't say I had a chest.*

*You didn't have to. A massive kinetic blast in the dirty docks wipes an Onyx tax skiff. Two hours later a Timberite starts asking about high-end fencing with the Mirlyns. I was waiting for whoever walked away with the payload to surface. You can't fence it closed. The Mirlyns will just kill you and take it.*

Eigor thumbs the edge of the screen. *And you can open it.*

*I was contracted by the security firm that designed the housing. Double-sealed. Harder than diamond. Pressurized caustic defense. You try to cut it, it melts the scripts and your face at the same time. Buy a heavy Skid. Come to the Peaks.*

Eigor closes the connection.

If Remo is lying, this is a trap with good grammar. If Remo is telling the truth, it is the only way the money comes out intact.

He takes his coat and goes back to the scrap-broker. He has a Skid to buy.

The ocean is flat and grey. The pirate runner's offset engine hums like a man talking in his sleep. At the helm, Vane leans on the steering column, checks the horizon, then looks over his shoulder.

Grisel is out on a pile of canvas near the stairwell. Three hours, no movement. Howling sleep is a serious country. You do not visit it without a reason.

Propped against the steel bulkhead, upright, bolted into the harness, is Brox's limbless torso. Feeding tube pulsing. Dual fans staring straight at the bridge, as if the bridge has done something personal.

Vane looks at the horizon. Looks back at Brox. Does a double take when he catches the control panel's reflection in the glass of the mask, two little worlds stacked in the optics.

He clears his throat.

"I, uh. I wish you the best, man."

The fans spin. Brox does not move. He cannot. That is the whole tragedy and the whole joke.

"I mean, I don't know how much you're tracking in there." Vane leans an elbow on the console. "Tess says the lights are on but nobody's home. Barrett says if the lights are on, somebody is paying the bill. Grisel seems pretty sure. And she's terrifying, so I'll take her word for it. I take most of my words from people who could throw me over a rail."

Silence. The good kind, if you are a torso. The itchy kind, if you are Vane.

"It's a tough break." He pulls a crushed pack of stim-gum from his overalls and pops a piece. "I almost lost my arm to a threshing gear a few years back. Not the same as having them all popped off by your girl. Obviously. But I get the vulnerability. You sit there and you think, if a wave comes over the bow, I am basically a very expensive hat."

He chews. The open ocean always makes him talkative. Isolation is a crowd if you let it be.

"You know, my cousin bought a Rhombus implant once. Just a baseline ocular upgrade. Said it would help him spot salvage. Damn thing shorted in a monsoon. Locked his vision on infrared. He spent three weeks trying to date a thermal exhaust vent because he thought it was a woman waving at him." Vane chuckles, then glances at Brox like the laugh might be rude. "Took us a month to afford the removal. He still asks about the vent sometimes. We don't let him near boilers."

He waits for a laugh.

Brox stares forward. Neck-clamp honest as a nail. The grill hisses a sterile breath.

"Yeah," Vane mutters. "You probably had to be there. Or you had to have arms. I'm starting to understand the arms thing."

Five minutes pass. Vane lasts almost four of them.

"The thing is," he starts again, fully turning to face the torso now, "I don't think Tess respects my mechanical input. She treats me like I'm just the mop guy. But I found the four-liter leak, didn't I? I told her, Tess, if you don't patch the starboard intake, we're going to list. And what did she say?" He throws his hands up. "She said, shut up, Vane. Can you believe that? Barrett laughed. Barrett laughs at weather. Barrett is not a reliable moral witness."

He leans closer. "Right? It's all ego with her. She's good. I'm not saying she isn't good. I'm saying I am also good, in a narrower, broom-adjacent way, and that should count."

A heavy clawed hand clamps his shoulder.

Vane yelps. Nearly leaves his boots. Spins.

Grisel is right behind him. Green eyes narrowed. Thoroughly unamused. Sleep still in the edges of her face, which somehow makes it worse.

"He thinks you're an idiot, Vane," she growls. "Keep your eyes on the water."

Vane swallows. Hands find the wheel like it is a church. He offers her a nervous, lopsided smile. "Right. Well. Good to know you speak for him now. Saves him the trouble. What with the... general situation."

Grisel does not smile back. She looks at Brox for a long second, and whatever she sees in the fans she keeps.

Vane clears his throat and points through salt-stained glass. "Good timing anyway. We're here."

Grisel steps to the console.

The eastern scrap-reefs pull themselves out of grey fog like a bad idea that got funded. An ancient pre-flood aircraft carrier, flight deck towering over the water, years of survival turning a military vessel into a floating rusted metropolis. The hull is stained with salt and overlapping territorial graffiti. Sparks rain into the ocean from men in makeshift harnesses, welding scrap plates along a decaying waterline. It looks like a city that learned to tread water and then forgot why it wanted land in the first place.

"Drop me at the lower gantry," Grisel says. She is already gathering her things. The conversation is over. The ocean can keep Vane.

She hoists the canvas bag. Kneels. Straps Brox to her chest. Neck-clamp to collarbone. Fans whirring against her throat, a second pulse.

Vane pulls the runner alongside a rusted pontoon. He does not ask for a goodbye. Tess appears in the hatch long enough to toss Grisel a small wrapped brick of dry ration.

"For the climb," Tess says. "Don't die in a way that makes us look bad. We just got this boat pretty."

"It is not pretty," Barrett calls from below.

"It's prettier than you," Tess calls back.

Grisel almost thanks them. She nods instead, which in her language is the same currency. She steps off and starts the long chug up the switchback stairs welded to the exterior hull.

Her legs burn. Her own bags would have been a conversation. Brox's chassis plus the dead weight of his limbs up eighty feet of vertical grating is a trial. She does it anyway. She does not stop. Stopping would give the bag time to feel like a body.

She reaches the top and steps onto the flight deck.

The noise is absolute. A bazaar packed shoulder to shoulder: scavengers, drifters, gang enforcers, Stillous rejects, all shoving through canvas tents and scrap-metal stalls. The air is roasting meat, exhaust, unwashed bodies. Grisel puts her head down and uses her shoulders. She has no idea where to go. She has no one to ask who would answer for free.

The canvas bag jerks.

She stumbles, spins, sees a filthy scarred hand vanish into the crush. The zipper hangs open. Brox's left cybernetic arm is gone.

"Hey!" The bark comes up from somewhere older than language. She unspools the flail and takes one threatening step. "Who took it?"

The crowd looks back with hollow, professional indifference. In a place like this, a high-end cybernetic arm is a year of rations. The thief is already a rumor.

Grisel's knuckles go white on the iron handle. She wants to crack skulls until the arm surfaces. She cannot risk a riot with Brox on her chest. She swallows the rage, pulls the torn bag around front, and clutches it against her stomach with the torso.

She scans for authority, which on a carrier like this means the person who can afford to stand still. Toward the aft, on the old command tower, a private terrace sits above the squalor. A Stillous stands at the rail. Tall even for his kind. Unnaturally lanky. Ashen grey scales. Deep jagged scars no healer bothered to mend, which among Stillous is either poverty or a statement. Grisel would bet on the statement.

Varnis.

She pushes through the last of the crowd and takes the metal ramp to the VIP deck. A baseline guard waits at the top, built like a brick wall, composite armor, heavy scattergun. He holds up a hand.

"I'm Griselda," she says, breathing hard.

He looks at her. At the torn bag. At the whirring limbless cyborg strapped to her chest.

"Huh," he says.

"I'm here to see Varnis."

"Good for you." He chews a matchstick. "Now step back before I throw you and your scrap-pile off the flight deck. I get a bonus for keeping the stairs pretty."

"A Timberite named Eigor sent me. Tell Varnis I have a Breaker. Tell him I know about the host-lock."

The chewing stops. His eyes go from her green skin to Brox's dual optics. He taps the bead in his ear.

"Got a Howling down here. Says she was sent by a Timber. Has a dismantled Breaker strapped to her chest... Yeah. Understood."

He steps aside and gestures at the steel door with the scattergun barrel, lazy, like ushering in bad weather.

"He's in a good mood today. Try not to ruin it. People keep ruining it. It's a whole problem."

Grisel adjusts the load and walks through.

The door shuts. The bazaar dies like somebody cut its throat.

Miles away, at the blind end of a forgotten Canary dock, Eigor pulls a canvas tarp over the Onyx chest and wrenches the ratchet straps tight. Two hundred kilos on a Skid. The lightcraft dips. The fins hover near the line that turns stealth into a dinner bell. He climbs into the elevated cab, primes the silent engine, and slips into fog.

On the carrier, the room is sparse. Varnis stands at a metal desk, piecing together a glass cylinder with the patience of a man who has outlived several kinds of urgency. He does not look up.

"I heard him tell you I was in a good mood. Don't get your hopes up. Eigor told me the issue."

He stops tinkering and turns. The ashen face is unreadable, which on a Stillous is almost a courtesy. He looks at the torn bag.

"He only has a right hand?"

"Someone snatched his left arm on the way to the door."

Varnis lets out a slow raspy sigh. "Savages. This is why I stay private. What did he look like?"

"No idea, really."

Varnis walks past her to a composite-armored guard. "Find the left arm. Snatch the arm of the guy holding it too. His own arm. I want a matching set. I want a lesson."

The guard nods and slips out.

Grisel shifts her stance. "So we're all in a bad mood. That's fine. You at least must have some good news. No reason to get the arm back unless you plan on putting him back together, right?"

Varnis pours a dark liquid, slams it, sets the glass down hard enough to make the cylinder parts jump. "Two things need to happen. Obviously I need the scripts Eigor promised. Second, I need the anti-host-lock."

He looks at her like she is a problem that arrived underdressed.

"I know if you had the money you would have offered it by now. That's no problem. If I expected it, I wouldn't be letting you waste my time. Your scrap pile here, and three of my best dudes down below, need that hardware. It should have arrived before you got here. Before Eigor even contacted me. I assumed he heard something."

A cold knot starts in Grisel's stomach. "What hardware."

"A secure transport." Varnis leans on the desk. "Double-sealed casing. Pressurized caustic defense. It was moving south through the dirty docks. Inside isn't just tax money, Griselda. The Empire confiscated the only working prototypes of the Breaker reversal tech. I paid a crew to hit the skiff. They went dark."

Grisel stops breathing.

Eigor did not steal retirement money.

He stole the cure.

And he is driving it to a Rhombus grifter in the borderlands so he can fence it to the one people on Earth who shoot visitors for sport.

Grisel keeps her face still. On a fleet you learn young that the worst thing you can do with a card is show it to the room.

She does not say the rest of it out loud. That the crate Varnis paid a crew to steal, the one that went dark, is the same crate Eigor bragged about hauling out of the dirty docks. That her friend is somewhere north right now, fencing the one thing on Earth that could put Brox back together, because he still thinks it's money.

"So you can't fix him," she says instead. "Not without the hardware."

"I can't fix anyone without the hardware." Varnis sets down his glass. "I have the rig. I have the hands. I'm missing the thing your Empire spent a fortune making and a fortune losing. Two things put your friend back together, Howling. The payment your Timberite promised me, and the box my people were bringing me before they stopped answering. Bring me one, we talk. Bring me both, we work."

"And if the box never turns up."

"Then your friend stays a passenger, and my three stay the same, and I stay polite about a loss I already paid for." He says it lightly, which is what makes it land. "Have Eigor contact me when he's ready to deliver. I don't chase couriers. I stay where they can find me."

Grisel looks down at Brox. The fans turn. The clamp holds his head where she put it.

"Hold on," she tells him. It's a stupid thing to say to a man with no arms and no vote. She says it anyway.

The ocean is pitch black.

Eigor runs the Skid at full throttle through incinerator territory. The fins slice clean. Hull up just enough. Wind howls through the open cab. His eyelids start to drop. Forty-eight hours collecting their bill. He slaps his own wooden face, forces the eyes open, pushes the throttle. A few more hours to the Triple Peaks. Then the chest opens, or he dies, or both, which is a kind of opening.

Out of the dark to the north: three quick flashes of violet.

His brain does not get the time it would need to name them.

The water around the Skid erupts.

Three Mirlyn seeker rounds punch the craft. The starboard fin shatters. The lightcraft spins, chassis buckling, nose digging into black water. Eigor is ripped from the cab. He hits the freezing turbulent ocean hard enough that even Timberite skin has an opinion.

The ruined Skid drifts.

The chest stays strapped to it, a dark, perfect rectangle, still closed, still heavy with the thing all three of them were willing to become monsters for.

And none of them, not one, knew what it actually was.

## CHAPTER 8: THE PIRATES OF ECHIDNA

The Mirlyn pirate ship cuts the black waves like a blade. It does not look built. It looks grown. Dark jagged iron. Composite sails. A silhouette that makes the night seem like it was designed around it.

On deck, the crew moves in absolute silence. Vile, vampiric figures: human survivalism cut with Stillous isolationist genetics and then cut again, until what is left is only the part that can last. Most have wide leathery wings grafted tight to their backs. Limbs replaced with sharp angular cybernetics meant for tearing, not gripping. They are the apex predators of the open sea. The only faction violent enough to keep the Onyx Empire in a conversation instead of a coronation.

Hanging from the lower yardarms are dozens of heavy, pulsing sacks.

The ship banks to intercept the drifting Skid. One sack tears. It hits the deck with a wet, heavy slap.

A few dozen Mirlyn newborns spill out, covered in thick amniotic sludge. Small. Pale. Feral. Jaws already working, already looking for a rule.

A winged crewman walks past. He does not offer a hand. He takes a heavy push broom and sweeps the squirming mass straight off the side.

Most of the newborns hit the water and sink. A few fight. Tiny vicious claws in rusted iron, screeching against the spray. The crewman watches two lose their grip and go into the wake. He does not count them. Counting would imply they had already become a number worth keeping.

One makes it over the rail. It hits the deck shivering, eyes wide and black. The crewman steps in, raises a boot, and chases the survivor into the dark under the stairs. The test is over. It has earned the right to live, which on a Mirlyn ship is not a gift. It is a temporary work visa.

Eigor breaks the surface once, bark-skin streaming, and sees the ship the way a man in the water is supposed to see a ship: as either rescue or appetite. The sails eat the stars. The crew does not call out. They do not throw a line. Mirlyns only scream when they go in for the kill, and they are not killing yet. They are collecting.

He dives again. Timberite lungs are not human lungs. He can stay down. He can think. The chest is still on the Skid. The Skid is still afloat, barely, a crippled insect on a stove. If the Mirlyns take the chest, the scripts are gone, the shuttle is gone, and whatever Varnis wanted out of that box is gone with it.

Eigor does not know about Varnis's box. He knows about months. He knows about Grisel on a carrier with a man in pieces. He knows about Brox's fans and the sound they make when they are the only part of him still doing a job.

He swims for the Skid.

Above, a Mirlyn with a cybernetic jaw steps to the bow and looks down at the crippled craft. No cheer. No speech. The captain raises a hand.

Boarding hooks drop.

They hit the Skid's frame with a sound like a trap closing on a leg that has already accepted it.

Eigor reaches the hull and gets a hand on a strap. The chest is right there. Two hundred kilos of locked future. His tendrils twitch in the palm, useless against metal, useless against a crew that does not need to be hijacked to be of one mind.

A hook yanks. The Skid lurches. Eigor's grip holds. A second hook yanks harder.

On the black water, the Mirlyn ship heaves the Skid up the hooks like a fish they are not hungry for yet. Eigor holds on because letting go is a different death, and he has not chosen one.

The chest rises with the wreck.

Closed.

Heavy.

Still pretending it is only money.

The captain of the Echidna-born tilts their head, listening to a silence the rest of the sea has never learned to keep, and the newborns under the stairs listen too, learning the first lesson their people will ever teach them:

Nothing that comes out of the water belongs to you until you can keep it from going back in.

## ARCHIVAL FRAGMENT: THE COMPETITIVE SPLIT (2447)

*Origin: Restricted addendum to the Lunar Sector 4 settlement guides, later copied into a Howling plant-archive under the heading they keep using for things that are not their business and will become their business anyway. A Stillous clerk stamped it: not a people. A method.*

There is a type of human that cannot stand a tie.

There is a type of Stillous that cannot stand a middle.

Those are not the same hunger, but they recognize each other. The human wants to win the comparison. The Stillous wants the comparison to stop existing. One writes a ranking. One writes a wall. Put them in the same drowned century and they will try to kill each other, which is already a regional problem. Teach them to stop killing each other and start iterating on the same body, and you do not get a compromise. You get a third species.

This is the part the older surveys get polite about. They say the Mirlyns “emerged from a middle caste of Rhombus and Howlings using Echidna archives.” That sentence is true the way a death certificate is true. It does not say why anyone would do it.

By the 2440s, both parent worlds still had a leak.

Human cohorts still grieved in public. They still made claims on each other that could be heard from the next raft. They still named children as if naming them made the water less interested.

Stillous cohorts still practiced privacy so complete that a neighbor could run an arena and nobody would knock. They also still fed their own weak. They still kept the unprofitable alive if the unprofitable belonged to the family. Isolation, in the old Stillous sense, had a soft floor. You did not petition outsiders. You also did not throw your own newborns off a deck to see which ones could climb.

The people who became Mirlyns looked at both floors and called them contamination.

If the universe only respects two states — absolute privacy, or absolute utility — then the winning move is to become something that cannot be petitioned. Not a cohort among cohorts. Not a company that takes children and calls it care. A species that does not have to explain the border because the border does not speak.

They took cybernetics the way both parent species already took them: as tools. Then they stopped treating the tools as decorations on an old body. Gene-work from the Echidna archives was not used to become Stillous. It was used to steal the parts of Stillous biology that make isolation viable. Toughness. Low need. Sack-birth. Wings that do not ask a dock for a ladder. A nervous system that does not plead as a first language. Human and animal traits went in the same vat: night sight, spite, the ability to hold a hull with the hands you have left. After a few generations the result was not a costume. It was not Earthling. It was not Stillous. It was Mirlyn.

They did not leave because they were oppressed. They left because both parent cultures were still available. Availability is how you get taxed. Availability is how a Breaker finds a neck. Availability is how a Stillous gang leader in the east sends a polite message and expects an answer.

Current politics, year 2668, as understood by anyone who is not allowed to see a Mirlyn port from the inside:

They hold two of Earth’s fifteen shuttle ports. That is not a kingdom. That is a valve. The live argument inside the walls is not good versus evil. It is whether a third valve would make them safer or make them rot. One flight says more ports means more control of Earth-to-Sol, which means fewer people knocking. Another flight says any extra contact is a smell. Trade already happens. It happens through gloves: Rhombus go-betweens, men like the ones who build junk fortresses on irradiated atolls and walk up to a wall without dying. You do not shake the dirty hand. You shake the man who shook it.

Status, among Mirlyns, is not a crown. Status is how little you need from outside. Competing walls. Competing litters. Competing flights. Cooperation exists. It exists inward, and inward only, and it is treated as a weapon aimed at everyone else.

A map of the interior is a kind of indecency.

A witness is a leak.

A Timberite with an Onyx chest is not a guest. He is a contamination event: Empire property, Stillous-relevant hardware, and a man whose talent is stealing a body and walking the memory out through somebody else’s mouth.

They will not kill him on the water if the box is still closed.

They will not let him see the door.

## CHAPTER 9: THE MATCHING SET

Varnis's room was quiet, but the carrier was not. Through the steel Grisel could hear the deck doing what it always did: thousands of people buying, selling, and threatening each other over things that would be worthless by next week. A crime lord's office was not a chapel. Twice while she waited, the door opened for other business — a runner with a manifest, a woman who needed a signature and did not look at Grisel or Brox or the bag, because looking cost time and time was the one thing nobody here spent on strangers.

Varnis handled them without raising his voice and went back to the glass cylinder on his desk as if she had not stopped being furniture.

The door opened again. This time it was the guard he had sent.

He came in carrying two arms.

One was Brox's. Pale-blue chassis, the left, the wrist coupling still smeared with the cheap grease Brox used because the good grease cost a Glove. She knew it the way you know your own name in a crowd.

The other was meat. Full flesh, no chrome, torn off at the shoulder. Still fresh enough that the room noticed.

The guard set both on the desk, careful, like he was proud of the set being complete.

Varnis looked at the metal arm. Then he picked up the other one by the wrist, turned it, and put it down.

"This one is full meat," he said. "And fresh."

The guard waited to be thanked.

"I said take the arm of the man who took it." Varnis wiped his fingers on a cloth. "I meant it as a figure of speech. A thing to pass along. He does not understand a figure of speech."

The guard's face did not change. He had done exactly what he was told, and that was the whole of his opinion.

Varnis slid Brox's arm across the desk. "This one is his. It's been rinsed. Put it with the others."

She packed it into the torn canvas with the rest of him. She did not thank Varnis. On this ship, gratitude was a way of admitting you had needed something, and she had needed too much already.

"When your Timberite surfaces, he knows the terms," Varnis said, already back at the cylinder. "Payment, and the hardware. I don't chase couriers. I stay where they can find me, and I let the ones who don't come back become a lesson for the ones who do." He glanced up once. "You've had a long day. I'll send someone to walk you down. The deck already ate one of his arms. I'd rather it not develop a taste."

She wanted to say she didn't need a walker. She looked at the meat arm on the desk, and at the noise pressing on the steel, and said, "Fine."

The guard who took her out was not the one who didn't understand hyperbole. This one carried the scattergun like a tool he was bored of and moved through the crush in a way that opened it. People stepped aside for the armor, not for her. She kept Brox high on her chest and let the man do the one thing he was good for.

Halfway down the switchback, where the pontoon stink came up through the grating and she could see water again, she stopped.

"Far enough."

He looked at her. "He said the ship."

"He said down. I'm down." She nodded at the runner, still tied to the lower gantry, still ugly, still there. "I walk up to my crew with your armor behind me, they'll think I got bought. Go tell him I made it."

The guard weighed it, which on this carrier passed for generosity, then turned and went back up without a word. She took the last stairs alone, legs burning in the old way, Brox's fans ticking against her throat.

"If Eigor's just drunk in Canary," she told the fans, because the climb was long and Brox was the only one who couldn't tell her to stop, "I'm putting him in the bag with you. You two can sort out the seating."

At the pontoon, Vane sat on a crate eating something that had stopped being a specific food. Tess had her head in an access panel. Barrett held a wrench the size of a child's leg and frowned at the sky like it had shorted him.

They looked up when her boots hit the deck.

"You're back," Vane said. "I had money on the fancy room."

"You didn't have money," Tess said, not lifting her head. "You had a feeling. Your feelings are why we're broke."

Barrett took Brox's bag out of Grisel's hand before she could pretend she didn't need that. He carried it like cargo, which was the kindest help he knew. "Mast or below."

"Mast. That's the deal."

"That's the deal," Barrett agreed, and set the torso against the base of the mast, and said nothing to it, which she noticed and was grateful for.

She sat with her back to the rail and thumbed her comms.

The first ping went out and landed in nothing.

## CHAPTER 10: OPEN WATER

Eigor did not answer that day, or the next.

The runner ran north-and-east under a sky the color of wet iron, and for the first time since Dundy, Grisel had nothing to do but be aboard a working ship. It was slower than she wanted and exactly as slow as the world actually was. Nobody who has spent real time on the water believes in fast. The horizon does not move for anyone. You point at it and keep pointing at it for days, and it has the decency not to pretend otherwise.

She stood watches because Tess put her on watches. She hauled line because Barrett handed her line. She learned the runner's noises: the offset piston that knocked twice and skipped, the starboard intake that whistled when the swell came from the north, the way Vane's boots sounded different when he was working versus walking somewhere to avoid it.

The crew got on with her the way working people get on with anyone who pulls weight — easily, and with nothing promised. Tess talked to her about valves. Barrett talked to her about nothing, which from Barrett was warmth. Vane talked to everyone about everything and to Brox most of all, because Brox was the only one who couldn't leave.

By the second evening it was clear the arrangement had an edge.

"Two days out there's a port that'll take the runner," Tess said. She said it to the engine, not to Grisel, which was how Tess said the things that mattered. "Ugly little place. Won't ask about the deck. We sell, we split, we all go be poor somewhere with a roof."

"And me."

"And you get off with your friend and your bag and our best wishes." Tess wiped a fitting. "We pulled you off a dead ship, greenie. That's a favor, not a marriage. We're not sailing into wall country because your Timber went quiet."

It was fair, and Grisel had heard it coming. On a fleet you learn to hear the exact moment a boat decides you're cargo instead of crew. This was that moment, said kindly, over an engine.

She had one card. She'd held it since the carrier, since the word cure, and she hadn't wanted to play it, because playing it made it real and made it theirs.

"There's money in it," she said.

Vane's head came up from the crate he was pretending to inventory.

"There's always money in it," Tess said. "Right up until there's a Wraithlion."

"Not this kind." Grisel kept her voice flat, because excitement was a thing you sold and she wasn't selling. "The chest my friend was hauling out of the docks — that's what the Onyx sweep was about. That's what put a Wraithlion on the last crew that touched it. It's not tax money. It's worth more than this runner. More than ten runners. It's the kind of number that ends with a shuttle."

The deck went quiet the way a deck goes quiet when a number lands.

Barrett spoke first, which was rare. "Worth that to who."

"To the people who already killed for it once. A Stillous on that carrier. The Empire. The wall people, if they've worked out what they're holding." She looked at him. "My friend had it. Now he won't answer, and the chest is wherever he is."

"So it's gone," Tess said. "Behind a wall or under the water."

"Or it's leverage." She'd turned it over for three days, in watches, in the dark. "Nobody who wants that chest has all of it. The Stillous knows what's inside and doesn't have it. The wall people might have it and not know what it is. The Empire wants it back and lost the trail. The only one on this ocean holding both halves — what it is and where it went — is sitting on your deck feeding a torso."

Vane whistled low. "That's either very valuable or very dead."

"Usually both, in order," Tess said. But she'd stopped wiping the fitting.

They argued it the way crews argue, which is badly and honestly. Tess wanted numbers she couldn't have. Barrett wanted to know who they'd have to fight and did not flinch at the answers, only nodded, filing each one. Vane wanted a cut written down where he could look at it, and a hat, which nobody addressed.

Grisel did not promise them the whole chest. A promise that big only tells people you plan to break it. She promised a share, real and specific, off whatever the chest turned into. She promised the runner stayed theirs either way. She promised to cover running costs until then — and that was the promise that cost her, because covering costs meant Gloves, and she had fifteen and no way to make more.

"Fifteen," Tess repeated, when Grisel laid two on the crate for fuel and salt at the next stop. "You're funding a war on Varnis's carrier with fifteen Gloves."

"Thirteen now."

"That's my point."

"I know what it is." Grisel put the sleeve away before the number could shrink from being looked at. "It's enough to find out where he is. After that it's leverage or it's nothing, and either way the Gloves were always going to run out. They always run out. I'd rather they run out pointed at something."

Barrett grunted. Agreement.

Vane looked at Brox, propped at the mast, fans turning. "What's his vote?"

"He'd tell me I'm bad at math," Grisel said. "Then he'd do the math himself, get the same answer, and pretend he didn't."

"You two grow up together?"

Idle, the kind of question Vane asked to fill air. She let it sit a beat longer than idle.

"No. I grew up on boats. He grew up owing people. Different schools." She checked the clamp, ended it with her hands. "Set me down at the next port that'll talk. I need a name."

"For what," Tess said.

"For whoever walks up to a wall and comes back with both arms."

They made the port on the fourth day. It was busy the way every surviving port was busy — too many people, not enough dry, everything for sale including the answer to a question, if you paid the man who happened to have it. Nobody there knew Grisel or cared to. She paid for salt. She paid for fuel. She paid three Gloves to a fixer who dealt in Mirlyn trade routes and did not once ask why a Howling wanted to know.

He gave her one name. He'd have given her a wrong name for less, so she made him say it twice, watched his face both times, and decided he was too bored to lie.

Remo. Triple Peaks. A Rhombus who walked up to the wall.

Ten Gloves left. A name. A course into dead water.

On a different water, a ship that did not need to answer anyone was already being waved through a gate.

## CHAPTER 11: THE UNSEEN PORT

From the air the port did not look like a place people lived. It looked like something poured and left to set.

Obsidian-dark walls came out of the northwest water in flat, even runs. No patchwork, no graffiti, none of the barnacled improvisation of every other settlement on the map. Mounted posts sat along the top at measured intervals, each one manned, each figure still in a way that had nothing to do with rest. The water inside the seawall was flat. Cranes moved on it slowly, deliberately, careful not to wake anything.

The pirate ship came in from the black with no horn and no chatter. A short violet pulse from the wall. The ship answered by changing its angle and nothing else. The gate opened because the wall decided to open it, and closed the same way.

Inspectors came out of a low structure that did not announce itself as a building. They crossed the boarding bridge in a line and went down into the belly of the ship without asking anyone to make room. The crew made room anyway. In the hold they moved along the strapped Skid wreck, checked the seals on the Onyx chest, checked them again, and did not open it. One of them crouched a long time in front of the last thing in the hold, which was Eigor, and looked at him the way a customs officer looks at a container that pinged wrong.

Then the height went away, because no one who wanted to keep breathing kept the aerial view of a Mirlyn port for long.

Eigor was bound at the wrists in a polymer that did not care what his hands could do. He had sat still for a day and a half because still was the only thing the hold rewarded. He had counted boots. He had counted the times they checked the chest and did not open it, which was eleven. He had not counted on rescue. He'd broken that habit as a child.

The inspector who had crouched stood and said something to two others in the clipped, click-heavy speech Eigor could not follow. One of them unfolded a square of heavy tarp.

They did not tell Eigor anything. They told the crew.

The tarp went over his head. Wool and oil and his own breath. The world shrank to fabric. A cord cinched at his throat, careful of the bark, the carefulness of people handling a tool they had not decided whether to keep. A hand took his shoulder and turned him.

"Walk," someone said, in trade-tongue, close.

He walked. The alternative was being dragged, and he'd had enough of that this week.

Ship-iron gave way underfoot to something denser, a surface that ate the sound of his steps. The air changed — no kelp, no exhaust, none of the rot that told you a place was alive with people. Cold, filtered, blank. A door sealed behind him in three separate motions.

He did not see the walls. He did not see the posts, or the flat water, or the fact that the place had a sky. That was the arrangement, and it was not for his benefit. A Timberite with a working hijack was a machine for stealing whatever he was shown, so they showed him a bag.

The hand stopped him. A voice, through the tarp:

"If you see, you die. If you reach for a neck, you die. If the box is open, say it now."

"It's closed," Eigor said.

A pause, like the word had to be filed before the conversation continued.

"Walk."

He walked. Somewhere behind him, eleven inspections deep, the chest went where he could not follow, still closed, still pretending it was money.

## CHAPTER 12: CONTAMINATION

They took the tarp off in a room with no windows and no clock, and Eigor understood that both absences were deliberate.

The walls were the same dark panel as the outside he hadn't been allowed to see. Low, even light. They sat him on a bench, not a chair — a chair is something you offer, a bench is something you put a body on — and left his wrists bound.

Two Mirlyns. One with a jaw rebuilt in metal, the one from the ship. One narrower, wings folded so tight against its back they read as a coat. Neither gave a name. Names were for things you expected to deal with again.

"You came off the incinerator lane in a Skid," the narrow one said. Not a question. A fact being checked against his face.

"Yes."

"With Onyx cargo."

"I don't know whose it is." A lie, a small one, offered to see what they already had.

The jaw moved once. The narrow one stepped in and hit him, open-handed, across the bark of his cheek, hard enough to turn his head. Not rage. Calibration.

"The locks are Onyx. The mint marks are Onyx. Half the Empire's southern patrol has been dragging the strait for it." The narrow one did not raise its voice. "We know whose it is. We ask whether you know, because a courier who doesn't know what he carries is a different problem than one who does."

Eigor worked his jaw. Timberite skin did not bruise the way meat did, but the joint rang. "I was paid to move it north. Told it was scripts. Tax money."

"By whom."

"A contact in Canary. He's dead." A safer lie. Flint had done nothing but pass a message, and a dead man couldn't be leaned on for a second answer.

The two of them looked at each other. The silence was not for him. It was them deciding, in whatever ran behind the clicks, how much of his story to bother testing.

"You are Timberite," the jaw said. The first time either had named what he was. "Full hijack. The tendrils."

Eigor said nothing.

"We have kept your hands bound and kept you off our people's necks since the water. That is not caution. It is procedure. We have handled your kind before." The jaw leaned in. "They do not leave with what they saw."

"Then let me leave without seeing anything," Eigor said. "You have the box. You don't need the man."

"We have a closed box and a man who steals bodies. Those are two problems, not one solution." The narrow one crouched to his level, which was worse than standing over him — it meant he was being examined, not threatened. "You will tell me who else knew you carried it. A crew. A woman. Anyone who comes looking, or comes talking."

Here was the hinge, and Eigor felt it give like a lock. Name Grisel — a Howling on a stolen runner with a broken Rhombus lashed to the mast — and he handed them a thread straight to the only two people the whole world came down to. Say no one, and if they found the thread anyway, they'd know he lied about the thing that mattered, and stop testing his lies gently.

"There was a crew on the extraction," he said. True, aimed sideways. "Sea-slums out of Dundy. Half of them died when the Onyx re-took the skiff. I don't know their names. I was hired hands, same as them."

"No one waiting for you."

"Everyone I'd wait for is already underwater or already paid." He held the narrow one's black eyes and did not blink, which for a Timberite was the same as breathing normally. "That's the job. You take work nobody misses if it doesn't come home."

Whether it believed him was written nowhere on its face, and Eigor understood that the blankness was the answer — they were not a people who let you read them. They had cut that out of themselves generations back.

The jaw said something in the click-speech. The narrow one answered, short. A decision, or the shape of one.

"You will stay," the narrow one told him. "You will not see a corridor that goes out. You will not be near a neck with your hands free. If you are useful, you will be told the use and nothing around it. If you are not, you will be told nothing, which is a kinder ending than you have earned."

They put the tarp back on to move him. He did not fight it. Fighting a procedure only teaches the procedure.

In the dark, walking, he reached out of old habit for the comms bead they'd taken out of his ear on the ship — quick, no discussion, the way you pull a splinter — and found the empty place where it had been. Grisel would be pinging into that silence right now. Calling him a coward out loud, because that was how she prayed.

He let her. It was the only cover he could give her from inside a bag, in a building he was not allowed to know the shape of: to be a hole she couldn't fill, so no one here would think to ask who was on the other end of it.

## CHAPTER 13: WHAT REMO KNEW

The Triple Peaks were three black teeth in boiled-looking water, and the runner had no business among them. Tess said so on the approach and kept saying it, which was how Tess prayed.

Remo's compound was welded onto a slab that had been a parking structure in some drowned century — gun nests, a Skid rack under a tarp doing worse than the Mirlyns' tarp ever had, antennae bristling at a sky that gave nothing back. The water around it was too quiet. Incinerator quiet. The kind of still that meant something under the surface was counting hulls by weight.

A voice hit their comms before a greeting did. *If that's a tax boat, drown where you are. If it's a sale, I don't buy runners. State it.*

"Griselda. I'm looking for Eigor. A Timberite. He was coming to you."

A silence that lasted long enough to be an answer. Then: *West cleat. Keep the virus on the deck where I can see it.*

Remo was a Rhombus who had kept more of his face than Brox and spent the savings on being unpleasant. Long coat, homemade rifle, a filter mask that was half fashion and half lung. He looked at Brox lashed to the mast, at Grisel, at Tess pricing his compound with her eyes.

"So you're his people," Remo said. "He didn't mention people. He mentioned a chest and a schedule, and then he stopped mentioning anything."

"When," Grisel said.

"Six days back he pinged me from Canary. Said he'd bought a heavy Skid and was coming up the incinerator lane to see me about a job." Remo rolled a shoulder. "He didn't come. Skids don't just sink on that lane — the swarm leaves the light ones alone if you fly them right, and your Timber flew them right or he'd never have made it out of Canary. What eats that lane is the wall people. They ran a boat through the night he was due. When they run, the swarm goes quiet, like even the bots know to hold still. That's what I've got. He was coming to me, he didn't arrive, and the Mirlyns were on the water the same night."

Grisel stood very still while the pieces slid together, because Remo had just handed her the last edges of a thing she'd been building for a week without knowing it.

Remo knew Eigor was coming to him. He did not know why — he'd been careful, in the way careful men are, not to ask what was in the chest. He did not know it was a cure. He did not know a Stillous had paid a crew to steal it. He did not know the crew Varnis paid was the same Onyx skiff Eigor had hit. He had one clean fact: the wall took the lane the night Eigor vanished.

She had the rest. She was the only place all of it lived at once.

She did not tell him. She'd learned that much on the carrier, watching Varnis hold his cards over a dark glass. You did not spread the picture around. The picture was the only thing she owned that everyone else was missing.

"Can you get a message to them," she said. "The wall."

Remo laughed, short and grinding. "I leave a crate at a spot and come back later to see if they left one. That's the whole relationship. I don't talk to Mirlyns. I trade with a door and hope the door's in a mood." Behind the mask, something sharpened. "You want me to leave a crate that says a Howling is asking after a Timberite they may be holding. Understand what that does. Right now, to them, you don't exist. The second I put your name on their doorstep, you exist. And existing is the one thing they kill for reliably."

"What's it cost."

"To leave the crate? Five Gloves, and I still might not come back — in which case the Gloves die with me and you learn nothing." He tilted his head. "That's if I do it. I haven't said I will. A man doesn't get old on this water carrying other people's names to a wall."

Five Gloves. She had ten. She thought about Brox at the mast, fans turning, and about how ten became five became none, and how none of it made a body move again.

"Don't leave the crate," she said. "Not yet."

The ridge where Remo's eyebrow used to be went up. "You come all the way into dead water and then don't spend."

"I came for what you knew. You told me." She was already turning the new shape of it over — the wall had Eigor and the chest and did not know what the chest was worth. "Knocking tells them there's something to hide. I don't want them curious. I want them bored, right up until I know what I'm doing."

Behind her, Tess said quietly, "And do you? Know what you're doing?"

"No," Grisel said. "But I know more than they do. That's a start."

Remo watched her the way you watch a card player who just refused a sure bet. "You didn't grow up trading. You've got fleet in your mouth. Fleet people think honesty is a personality." He almost sounded approving. "Whatever you're building, build it fast. The wall doesn't stay bored. Somebody in there is already asking what's in that box. When they answer their own question, your Timber stops being a courier and starts being a witness. You know what they do with witnesses."

"I'm learning," Grisel said.

She paid him one Glove for what he'd told her, because information you don't pay for gets remembered as a debt, and she wanted no debts in dead water. Nine Gloves. A friend behind a wall. A chest nobody else understood. And, for the first time since Dundy, something under the panic that was almost useful: the cold, plain knowledge that she was standing in the middle of a thing no one else could see all of.

## CHAPTER 14: THE ARITHMETIC

They pulled off the Peaks before the swarm could reconsider them and lay to in open water a half-day south, engine ticking down, the black teeth sinking into the horizon behind them.

Grisel laid it out for the crew, because they had bought in, and a crew that has bought in is owed the real picture or they start inventing worse ones. She sat them at the base of the mast, Brox propped where he was part of it, and counted it off on her claws like a manifest.

"Four people want that chest, or would if they knew to want it. The Stillous on the carrier knows exactly what's inside — a Breaker cure, real, the only working kind. He doesn't have it, and he can't fix Brox without it. The Empire wants it back and lost the trail at the docks. The wall people have it and don't know what it is yet. And Eigor's inside the wall with it, which means the one man who could open his mouth is behind the one door nobody opens."

"And you," Barrett said.

"And me. I know what it is, where it is, and what everyone's missing." She let that sit. "That's what I'm bringing to this. Not Gloves — I've got nine. Not a crew that can storm a Mirlyn port; no crew can. Just that I'm the only one holding the whole picture, and everybody else is trying to make a deal with half of it."

Vane, for once, was quiet a moment before he spoke. "That's not a plan. That's a fact about you."

"It's the start of one." She looked at him, at Tess, at Barrett, at the water. "Every one of those players would pay for the half they're missing. The wall would pay to learn what they're really holding — except telling them makes Eigor a witness, so that door's shut. The Empire would pay to learn it's behind a Mirlyn wall — except pointing Onyx at that wall gets everyone inside it killed, Eigor with them. The Stillous would pay the most. He needs the cure, he can't reach it, and he'll deal with the wall through men like Remo without ever leaving his carrier. Varnis is the lever. He wants it badly enough and he's far enough from it to need me."

Tess wiped her hands slowly. "So you sell Varnis the location of a chest that's behind an obsidian wall. And in exchange he fixes your man and pays us."

"He wants two things to fix Brox. Payment, and the hardware. The hardware is the chest. Put the chest in his hands and the payment's a formality — he'll waive half of it just to hold the only cure on Earth. That's the number that ends with a shuttle. That's your share."

"There's a hole in it," Tess said, because Tess found holes for a living. "You still have to get the chest out of the wall. You just spent a speech explaining nobody gets into the wall."

"I know." Grisel didn't look away from it. "That's the part I don't have. That's the part I go get, before I'm out of Gloves and Brox is one bad repair from a torso that stops twitching." She put her hand on the clamp, the way she used to put it on his coat in a crowd. "But I know what I'm carrying now. A week ago I was a Howling with a bag of arms and no idea why the world was chasing me. Now I know exactly why, and I know I'm the only one who does. On this water, that's almost everything."

Barrett nodded, slow, the way he nodded at bad odds he'd already decided to take. "So where."

Grisel looked southeast, toward a rusted carrier and a Stillous with a glass cylinder and a fondness for unfinished work.

"Back to Varnis. Not to sell yet. To find out what he'd give to get his chest back, and what he knows about a wall that I don't. He's older than this ocean. If there's a way in, he's watched somebody try it."

"And if there's not?" Vane asked.

"Then I've still got the one thing nobody can take unless I say it out loud." She checked the clamp a last time and stood, and the crew stood with her — because a payday was a payday, and because somewhere in the last four days, without any of them deciding to, she'd become a person they'd sail with. "Get us moving, Tess. Long way back, and I've got a lot to work out before we're in his water again."

The runner came about and put its ugly nose toward the southeast. Grisel sat with Brox at the mast and did the arithmetic again, the way you worry a knot: nine Gloves, one cure, one wall, one friend behind it, one friend beside her who couldn't speak, and one fact only she could see — worth more than all of it, if she could work out how to spend it without getting them all killed.

The horizon didn't move for her. It never did. But for the first time since the docks, she was the one deciding which part of it to point at.
