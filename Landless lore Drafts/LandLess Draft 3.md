## CHAPTER 1: PORT DUNDY

A few miles out from the port, Brox climbed down to the lower deck to bring Grisel food and tell her they were almost somewhere she could sleep.

Midday to near-black in four steps. He hit the first stair wrong and the servo in his right knee cracked loud enough that a passenger above swore at the noise. He did not apologize. He never did. He went down into the underbelly of the ship, past the table saws bolted to the floor so they would not walk in a swell, past the two big water tanks, to the place where the hull curved in and the dark got honest — because that was where she would be.

She was folded against the steel, one clawed hand hooked through a pipe so the roll of the ship could not dump her onto the grating. Green skin gone almost black in the low light. A broken horn on her forehead catching a thread of leak-light from a seam above. She had been down here the whole trip. She always was, this far south. The sun did not like her and she did not like the sun, and neither of them had ever been willing to talk it out.

He crouched and rubbed the edge of her ear with the two fingers on his left hand that were still meat. Set a crispybar on her chest. Leaned a cold sunshake against her hip.

"A few miles to Dundy," he said.

Grisel heard the fans before she felt the food — the twin intakes set over his eyes, spun up fast from the climb. She always heard them before she saw him, the way you hear a kettle before it screams. She rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand, sat up, cracked the shake, and drained half of it in one pull. Then she leaned in and kissed the filter grill where his mouth used to be, which was as close as she got to his mouth in public. Or in private. Or ever.

"I just want a room this time," she said. "A real one. A door that closes. Where I don't wake up every five minutes to a crash, or a scream, or some idiot dropping a wrench on a tank two feet from my head."

"That last one was me."

"I know it was you. I'm being generous."

His voice came out of the vocalizer flat and scraped, the grill hissing on every breath he could not take on his own. "Eigor got in ahead of us. Found a hole a few blocks off the import wing."

"Of course he did." She wiped her mouth on the back of her wrist. "Eigor arrives. Eigor finds a hole. Eigor texts us like we should be grateful he remembered we exist."

"He remembered the drinks."

"He remembered the drinks because he wants something." She looked at him then — really looked. The burlap-colored filter mask. The two fans over the eyes. The central grill. "You running hot?"

"Humidity."

"You always say humidity."

"It's always humid."

She let it go, the way she had learned to let it go, and ate the crispybar in the dark while the ship carried them in.

They waited until the hull kissed the dock and the other passengers thumped off overhead in a herd. Grisel had dodged the sun the whole trip; she would hold off being blinded as long as the steel let her. When the deck went quiet, Brox shouldered his pack and picked up his carry case. She took her own case and the little purse she swore was not a purse, put one hand over her eyes and the other flat on the back of his coat, and let him walk her up.

The south hit her like a sheet of heated glass. She stopped. Brox did not let her stop.

"I can't see a thing," she said. "I feel like I could walk right off into—"

"I've got you." The mechanical left hand closed on her elbow and stayed there. "Long time since you've been this far south."

"I remember why."

"You remember the hangover. Not the sun."

"I remember both. The hangover had the decency to end."

They came down the boardwalk into Port Dundy, a city built the way everything got built now — rafts and slabs lashed to whatever floated, neighborhoods divided the way a wreck divides into rooms: by whoever got there first and nailed something down. Some of the wood was older than anyone standing on it, and nobody had been stupid enough to replace it. Water moved under the planks the whole time, close, patient. People fell through the cracks here. Sometimes literally — keel-hauled and dragged under the old slabs. Other times they just went missing, which was the polite way of saying the water took a vote.

Every kind of animal walked the city like it had a shift.

A woman with something giraffe in the long stretch of her neck sold filtered water out of a drum. Two ordinary human kids chased a rat the size of a dog, and the rat was winning. A knot of figures with bark for skin moved through the crowd in a tight, silent column, dull as old furniture, not looking at anyone they did not have to look at. Nobody looked back at them. In Dundy you learned fast what to look at and what it would cost you to look at.

People stepped a little wider around Grisel. They always did. A green-skinned woman with a broken horn and claws that hooked pipes — half of them reached, without quite deciding to, toward whatever they kept on their belt. She kept her eyes down against the light and let Brox be the thing they watched instead.

The three of them did not belong to a company and did not pretend to. Eigor made the money and carried the other two most months. Brox and Grisel took the work that did not need a face people wanted to see, or lungs that worked in open air, or a woman like Grisel walking into a shop without somebody reaching for a weapon. What Eigor did for the money was not the kind of thing anyone said out loud in daylight. It was enough to know that his own people — the bark-skinned cohorts, private, stoic, who explained themselves to no one — still let him take contracts. And they did not let anyone do anything unless it was rare, and it was worth it.

They found him at an outdoor bar on the import-side edge, where the boardwalk was wide enough that a fight could happen without putting anyone in the water by accident. Eigor had a table already. A drink already. And the look he got when the last job was still sitting in his mouth and he had not decided whether to spit it out. His bark-skin was smooth as sanded wood, dark at the neck like it had been rained on. He did not sweat. His kind never did.

Brox dropped his carry case and sat. The chair complained. Grisel sat beside him, still squinting, one hand up like a visor.

"You're late," Eigor said.

"We're on a passenger hauler," Brox said. "You're on whatever you stole."

"Rented."

"Sure."

Eigor sipped. "How was the hold?"

"Dark," Grisel said. "Which is the only nice thing I have to say about this latitude. You get the room?"

"I got a lead on a room."

"That is not a room."

"It's a better sentence than no."

Brox leaned forward, the fans over his eyes ticking up a notch. "How'd the job go?"

Eigor looked at his drink like it might grow a door. "We can talk about that later."

"That's what you said last time."

"Last time, later was later. This time, later is still later."

They drank. A few hours went by, and a few dozen drinks between the three of them, which was not the same as a few dozen each but close enough that the difference stopped mattering. The bar filled with dock noise. Somebody two tables over lost at cards and started explaining why the cards were wrong.

Brox was listing. Not falling. Listing. He jabbed a finger at Eigor.

"You gonna pass out like a tourist, you crusty shitstain?"

Eigor laughed and half spit his drink. "Not tonight. Pretty sure if I woke up I'd be lucky to have a shoe left." He turned the glass in his hand. "Let's go to the Daisy District."

Brox made a sound that might have been a laugh if the mask let it be one. "We're both about to fall asleep. Why would I go anywhere near there. Especially with Grisel."

Grisel pointed at him without looking up from her drink. "Thanks, Eigor. But he's right. I'd much rather sleep than blow my ears out."

Eigor pulled two stims from his coat and set them on the table like he was dealing a hand he already knew the end of. "Ship leaves tonight. Take one of these, you've got twelve hours once we board. I want to talk to you about the job. And the next one." He tapped them. "I've already got eighty Glove off the last one. I'll toss you fifteen either way."

Behind the fans, Brox's eyes did the rare thing they did. They widened. He covered it with the glass.

Fifteen Glove was a room with a door that locked. Fifteen Glove was a month of not taking the jobs that needed Grisel to walk into somewhere and smile at people who were already reaching. She felt the number land in her chest, and she hated that it landed.

"Brox is already fried and I'm not even browned," she said. "You buy the drinks."

"Sure."

"And carry my stuff."

"Fine."

"And the room. After. A real one. Door. Lock. No wrench."

Eigor looked at Brox. "Why's she like this after a nap?"

"She's like this after a nap, before a nap, and during the part where she pretends she might nap." Brox picked up a stim, turned it over, and pocketed it. "Daisy District. You talk, we listen. If the talk is stupid, I'm eating this and sleeping on your floor anyway."

"It's not stupid."

"You always say that too."

Eigor stood, collected Grisel's carry case without a word, and started walking. Brox and Grisel followed him into the part of Dundy that did not care what you were, as long as you could pay to be loud.

Behind them, the bar kept going. Ahead, the boardwalk narrowed, and the water under the slabs sounded closer, like it had been listening the whole time.

They walked on, and the city folded its noise down to the hum it keeps for the middle of the night. None of it was new. All of it had a beginning, if a person cared to go back far enough — the horns, the Gloves, the green cast of Grisel's own skin. Most people didn't care to. The water had a long memory and no reason to share it.

> ### ◆ ARCHIVE — THE NUCLEAR CAMPFIRE (2098)
>
> *Recovered briefing, Freechron Internal / later circulated as public myth. Dates verified against plant-commission logs.*
>
> The late twenty-first century solved energy the way it solved most problems: by building more of the thing that had already almost killed it. Advancements in nuclear plants made small-community reactors viable. A town could run its own fire. The benefits were obvious. The security problem was not.
>
> Most of the risk got sanded down with efficiency, automation, and isolation. Reactors were put where people were not. Cameras watched the cameras. Still, there were tasks automation could not finish. A valve that had to be felt. A smell that was wrong before any sensor called it wrong. A human being who would fall asleep, or take a bribe, or decide the alarm was a drill.
>
> In 2098, a lot of those concerns became a thing of the past.
>
> The late 2100s had no shortage of bioengineering companies. Almost all of them were trying to improve the human body. Stronger bone. Cleaner blood. Longer life, if you could pay for the years. Only one company spent serious research on uplifting.
>
> Freechron.
>
> Freechron did not want a better human. Freechron wanted a better animal. Lifespan. Intelligence. Strength. Dexterity. Their first successful, replicable gene-set is what ended up solving most of the plant security problem, not because that was the goal, but because the goal and the problem happened to fit in the same mouth.
>
> What became lovingly known as the Howlers started as a new type of dog.
>
> They were smart enough to do the plant work. They were given thumbs. Their senses were pushed until a Howler could hear a hairline crack in a coolant line from the other side of a containment wall. They did not get bored in the way people got bored. They did not look at a reactor and see a career. They saw a fire that had to be watched.
>
> Howlers were treated as authority almost immediately, and not because anyone voted on it. They were dedicated. They could not be bought with the usual currencies. Within a short time they were deployed into almost every plant that could afford the contract. Within a few years there was no need for any humans on site at all.
>
> They could sense anyone inside the perimeter. They were the most trusted workers on the grid. They enjoyed the work. They got nothing but respect, which, for a while, was true enough to print.
>
> It started to sound inevitable, the way people talk when they want a decision to feel like weather. Man's best friend should be the one watching the fire. The natural next evolution of the dog. A campfire story with a payroll.
>
> What the public briefings left out was the part Freechron already knew: once you hand a species a job that important, and you make them better at it than you are, you do not get to take the job back. You only get to hope they still like you when the fire gets bigger than the camp.

## CHAPTER 2: BOOTY CALL

Eigor handed the fifteen Glove to Brox on the walk to Daisy — sealed in a glass sleeve, fast, like he was afraid Brox would sober up enough to say no if he waited.

Brox did not have many faces. His eyes did the thing anyway. They went wide and stayed wide while he shoved the sleeve into his bag, half stumbling, suddenly a little more awake, because money does that even when the lungs don't.

"We should go to the loud place and tell some stories, then," he said, the words scraping out of the vocalizer, the grill hissing between them.

Brox was an engineer — a decent one, and not much else. He wore the long coat because open sun would take his skin off in a day; his kind bruised at the light. His own people lived up in the dead waters and he couldn't stand them. He hadn't been raised by them and didn't miss the schooling. They looked at a living body, he'd said once, and saw a prototype nobody had bothered to finish. He'd rather drink with a Timberite and a Howling than sit in a room full of that, and he'd made the choice loudly enough, young enough, that it had stopped being a choice and started being who he was.

The three of them cut toward the Daisy District — venues, call girls, music that started in your teeth. Leaving the bar they passed sleeping transients, shacks nailed to shacks, stretches of flooring where the wrong step was a thirty-foot drop into black water. Sixteen miles of it, Dundy, rafts and huts and old civic slabs still pretending to be a plan. Great place to make money. Great place to get stabbed in an alley. Sometimes the same afternoon.

Grisel walked with her hood up even though the sun was finally dropping. The broken horn still made a shape under the fabric. A couple of dock kids stared. She stared back until they found something else to be brave about.

"You didn't have to flash the whole sleeve in the street," she told Eigor.

"I flashed it to Brox. The street can mind its business."

"The street is made of people who mind other people's business for a living."

Brox adjusted the bag against his ribs. "Somebody wants to rob a Timberite, a Howling, and a man who sounds like a dying generator, they can try. I'll even hold the bag open."

"Don't joke that where I can hear it."

"You can hear everything."

"That's what I just said."

Twenty minutes in they found a nightclub doing the most honest work in Daisy: making it too loud to think and too dark to judge. They walked in. Grabbed drinks. Eigor looked for a table. Grisel caught the first bars of a song she liked and her whole body changed, like somebody threw a switch in her spine. She threw her hands up, grabbed Brox, and hauled him onto the floor, nearly spilling his drink.

He smiled. The mask hid most of it. She could still tell. She always could. She knocked her shoulder into his chassis hard enough that the servo complained, and laughed when it did.

Eigor appeared at the edge of the floor with two more glasses and the face of a man trying to be a person for five more minutes.

"That stim kicking in?"

"I'm wide awake now," Brox said.

"Glad to hear it. I needed you two alert for a reason." Eigor leaned in so he didn't have to shout. The bass made his voice come up through the floor. "I've got a full Onyx war chest in transit."

Brox went still. The fans over his eyes jumped. "That your last job?"

"Let's sit down for this one." Eigor glanced at the bar, at a pair of expensive coats he decided were just men who wanted to look expensive. "Grab another first. Get loose."

They found a table on the edge, the kind that exists so people can pretend they came to talk. Grisel put her back to a pillar. Brox took the seat that watched the door. Eigor sat like a man who'd already counted the exits and hadn't liked the number.

"I'm doing an extraction," he said. "A thing on one boat. It needs to be on another."

He told it the way he told most things — from the middle, then backward, then the part that mattered. He'd been half deep into Timberite water, floating tree-compounds sitting on their own green islands like they'd never heard of the rest of the world. A crew was already in position. His job was the part nobody else could do: a chest in a room against the hull, one Glove-charge, maybe one guard, maybe three. For the professional use of the thing in his hand, they were going to pay him enough to keep all three of them fed for months.

He ran the whole approach for Brox in that low gravel he used when a story stopped being a story — the net, the rope on his foot, getting dragged under the hull, the climb, the hole cut small, the guard who came to check the leak. The slice at the back of the head. The connection. Mounting the guard's own rifle from inside the guard, walking a meat-shield down a hall while Onyx cannons went off outside, blowing the door.

"Inside is a locked Onyx war chest. Two hundred kilos, easy." He turned his glass. "I shoved my charge in the guard's mouth, kicked him off me, and he blew a hole through the hull. Grabbed the chest strap on the way down. The crew bridged the water and caught me."

He tapped the sticky table.

"Half that ship was Onyx. The other half was sea-slum labor. Tax collection, moving south. I've seen those lock mechanisms. That's clean, Earth-minted scripts in there." He let the club noise fill the gap. "That's retire money. That's shuttle money."

Brox wiped his mouth under the mask — a habit that did nothing and never had. "Who in the north pays a tax to the Empire?" He raised a hand before Eigor could answer. "Forget it. I know where this goes. You don't want your cut from the crew. You want the whole chest."

Eigor didn't deny it. That was how Brox knew.

"They're in the side docks. Leaving in four hours. Twelve crew on a heavy boat." Eigor leaned in. "But here's the part. Word is the ship already got raided and taken by a small Onyx crew. No way they didn't recover the chest. No way they don't run the strait to get back on schedule."

"Why do you say that like it helps," Brox said.

"You agreed to hear the plan."

"I agreed to hear the plan because you put fifteen Glove in my bag and a stim in my blood. That's not the same as me being smart."

Eigor almost smiled. "I need someone to bypass the dock anchors while I handle the crew. And someone who can pilot a skiff fast enough to clear the sector before the Empire or the Timbers work out what happened."

Brox looked at his hands. One metal. One not. He looked at Grisel. The quiet between them weighed more than the music.

"I get it," he muttered. "But it doesn't matter how well I know the tech. I go down there, it's out of my hands. It's a coin toss." He touched Grisel's arm, gentle, like it might break if he meant it. "You can't come. You stay back."

Grisel stared at her drink. Neon crawled across the amber. She thought about the endless damp of lower decks. The scraping outside a hull that kept her awake. The thing that had taken her family, dragging them down through the dark, the sound of it still living in her teeth. She thought about what shuttle money meant. Thick walls. A door that closed. A place where the dark didn't have a current.

She swatted his hand away. Her jaw was tight.

"Stay back where, Brox? Here? Waiting to find out if you got your throat cut? Waiting for some transient to kick me off a thirty-foot drop while you're gone? I came south so I wouldn't sit in a hole and listen. Don't put me back in the hole and call it love."

Brox's fans stuttered. "I didn't say love."

"You never say it. You just do that thing with your hand and hope I translate."

She looked at Eigor. Her hands were shaking. She flattened them on the table.

"You really think this buys us off the water?"

"I do."

"Say it like a person."

"I think that chest is the first thing we've ever touched that isn't just another month. I think if we don't take it, somebody else will, and we'll still be sleeping over water, and you'll still wake up every time a hull makes a noise." He turned his glass. "I also think if I do it alone, I die, and you two split my coat. So. Yes. It buys the shuttle."

Grisel swallowed the knot in her throat. She was not a fighter, not the way they'd need one, and she knew she was the weakest link at the table. She also knew the other option. Waiting to die in the dark was still dying.

"I'm driving the skiff," she said. It came out hard, like she'd had to bake it that way. "And we split it even. Let's go."

Brox started to argue. She was already standing. Eigor was already counting the hours they had left. The song changed. Nobody at the table heard the new one.

They finished their drinks the way people finish anything once the next thing is already decided — slowly, without tasting it. Four hours until the docks. For now there was only the noise and the neon and three people not talking about the water waiting under the floor. It had been waiting a long time. Longer than the city. Longer than the Gloves that lit it. Longer than the whole drowned arrangement that had made a night like this possible.

> ### ◆ ARCHIVE — THE FIRST MESSAGE (2130)
>
> *Compiled from open-frequency logs, Sol Broadcast Archive, and later Stillous-authorized translations. Popularly called First Message Day, though the message itself was not a day. It was a week of people pretending they had always expected it.*
>
> Everyone can remember where they were when first contact was made. That is the sentence people use, and it is only half true. The message was a footnote in the tabloids one day. The next day everybody was talking like they had been waiting their whole lives to be spoken to.
>
> The message was clear. Open. Anyone could read it.
>
> They called themselves Stillous.
>
> An alien race confirmed they were heading toward Earth on a generation ship that had already been traveling for twelve hundred years. It took a decade for them to even read the human response. Then nine years for Earth to hear them answer. For the next hundred years the delay got shorter and shorter, like a long conversation walking into the same room.
>
> They were given a region of the moon. Their own land. Close enough to humans to trade and far enough to sleep. They came from a planet with tougher conditions and less atmosphere. Earth air was rich to them. Earth gravity was a suggestion they learned to live with.
>
> When they departed their home, they did not know about humans. They did not expect us. They knew only that Earth showed the signs of a world that could hold life. They did not realize anyone was already here until around the 2050s. They spent time debating whether to turn back. They decided to make contact instead. They had plenty of years to learn the languages and the history before they said hello.
>
> The Stillous and the humans were alike enough to make agreements, and excited enough to mean some of them. There were many small differences. A few major ones.
>
> The strangest thing for humans was their recorded history.
>
> The Stillous development from dormant and animalistic to advanced intelligence was all saved. It was not myth. It was footage, logs, tool-marks, the whole ugly staircase. Beside the Stillous, one other alien species existed in their oldest records. Some said that species was extinct. Some said it was very far away. The Stillous knew almost nothing about them except this: they were the ones who raised the entire Stillous civilization out of pre-tribal nature.
>
> Ancient probes had landed on the Stillous homeworld with advanced uplifting tech. Early Stillous learned to work the terminals. They made tools they should not have been able to make. The probes dropped in the thousands. Most still existed and were still in use centuries later. Some had functions the Stillous had never learned. Throughout their existence, the origin of their intelligence was known and available. They could watch themselves get made.
>
> They had assumed, reasonably, that whoever sent the probes had been lifted by someone older, and that someone by someone older still, a chain of raised hands going back to the first possible mind. Then they found humans.
>
> Humans had done it alone.
>
> That fact did more damage to Stillous philosophy than any weapon Earth owned. Where Stillous had certainty, humans had religion. Where Stillous had a recorded parent, humans had a gap and a thousand stories stuffed into it. The Stillous on the ship were in a strange position: they had come to pass along the torch, to find a living world and uplift whatever was there. They had thought of that as a sacrifice. A duty. Then they arrived and the animals were already building cities and lying about why.
>
> The homeworld would not hear any of this for centuries, and would not be able to answer for centuries after that. The mission was not going to play out the way it was planned. The biggest advantage the travelers had was how excited and accepting the humans were. Excitement is a kind of blindness. The Stillous were polite enough not to call it that.
>
> They were not one people. The ship held a full, messy slice of their cultures, the most adventurous of every kind. They came in all shapes and sizes, from a few feet tall to nearly eight, scaly skin in every pattern, some natural, some designed. Bipedal. Two arms. Many of them tall and slouchy by nature. They all had faces that looked, to humans, almost like a smile. They often said humans had a face that looked like we were always about to eat.
>
> Their genders divided into two working categories: supplementive and maintenance. There were a few dozen forms of genitalia, six associated with the supplementive gender. In nature, supplementives provided nutrients to the colony. Builders. Hunters. Town security. Maintenance genders kept the newborns alive. They gave birth in cycles, in hut-like structures with a hard outer shell and inner incubation chambers. Some fed the body. Some maintained it directly. Others handled birth cycles and the long work of keeping an immobile infant from dying. Stillous babies could not move for a few years. They needed constant attention. They reached adulthood around thirty Earth years. Natural lifespan sat around two hundred. Their biology was tough and easy to modify. On the ship, many had already learned how to push that number until death became more of a decision than a date.
>
> About eighty percent of the ship's population had been born in transit. They knew nothing of the homeworld except stories and archives. Most spent long stretches in stasis, taking turns waking for maintenance and child-rearing. About six hundred thousand total. Of the original one hundred sixty thousand that left, only around eleven thousand had died. Many by choice. A few by accident. The ship was huge and getting crowded. A lot of them opted to stay asleep.
>
> They started giving Earth information and technology. Major advancements. One of them was an efficient, compact energy source: almost near-complete stable energy in its densest form.
>
> They called it a Glove.
>
> A small one could be picked up in a hand, if the hand did not mind a few volts. Like a battery, except it output a constant charge. They came in sizes from a dime to a small boat. A Glove would hold a consistent voltage for a few thousand years. They could be combined. They did not care if you used them to light a room or run a city.
>
> Earth did not understand, not at first, that a gift that clean always has an exhaust. The Stillous understood. They just did not think the exhaust was their problem.

> ### ◆ ARCHIVE — THE IDEOLOGY SCHISM (2412)
>
> *Excerpt, Sol Historical Survey, Volume VII: Out of Context Events. Circulated in lunar academies; banned in two Mirlyn cohorts; popular in Dundy bars as the thing people quote when they want to sound like they read.*
>
> The timeline of Earth's collapse is not defined by a single war. Historians call the sequence the Out of Context Events, which is a polite way of saying the planet was asked a question it did not have the language to answer.
>
> The Stillous generation ship did not arrive as conquerors. It arrived as indifferent benefactors. Their moral framework was born of extreme age and complete historical clarity. They knew exactly who had seeded their intelligence. They had outgrown religion, or at least they had outgrown the need to call it that. Sentimentality was a human habit they found interesting and nonessential. Earth, to the original vanguard, was a logistical waypoint. A planetary substrate. A place to stand while the next part of the mission got organized.
>
> Then the Gloves went to work.
>
> Stillous-gifted Glove plants reacted with Earth's atmospheric pressure and began outputting hyper-dense water vapor as a byproduct of near-infinite energy. The oceans rose. Not in a movie way. In a math way. Year by year, coast by coast, the map became a suggestion.
>
> The Stillous did not intervene.
>
> Their biology was resilient. Adaptable to harsh atmospheres and deep pressures. The flooding of a terrestrial world was a negligible variable in a macro-system. They had come to uplift, and the species they found was already intelligent, so the mission became maintenance, and maintenance did not include pumping a planet dry because the natives had built their cities too close to the tide.
>
> This indifference birthed the Schism.
>
> As decades passed, Stillous born in the Sol system — who had never seen Echidna, never stood under Typhon's light, never touched the old probes except as museum objects — began to fracture.
>
> Traditionalists wanted to expand the clinical empire across the drowned Earth. Keep the mission. Keep the distance. Treat the water as a new floor.
>
> Separatists, stained by human culture, rebelled and formed sympathetic enclaves. They learned jokes. They learned guilt. Some of them even learned to lie about why they were helping.
>
> Others abandoned the grand mission entirely. They took Stillous tech into the open water and became piratical warlords, because a tool does not care what kind of hand holds it, and a Glove does not ask if you are building a hospital or a throne.
>
> Humanity retreated as the continents went under. The nuclear plants and the Glove reactors were left to the Howlers. Freechron's uplifted dogs remained at their posts. They were the last line of defense against total ecological annihilation, and they knew it, because knowing it had been bred into them as surely as the thumbs.
>
> When it became clear humanity could no longer protect the plants — or the Howlers — the Howlers took command of the remaining bio-engineering bays. They did not hold a summit. They did not send a letter. They opened the tanks and started working.
>
> They synthesized their genome with other mammalian lines. Apes. Giraffes. Bears. They integrated cybernetics aggressively enough to survive a corrosive, flooded world that had never been designed for any of them.
>
> By the 2600s they were no longer just the plant workers.
>
> They were the Howlings.
>
> A massive, wildly diverse faction of human-animal-machine hybrids. Fiercely neutral. Deeply loyal to their own. Engineered to endure the world the Stillous had accidentally broken, and uninterested in thanking anyone for the accident.
>
> On Earth, people still told the campfire story. Man's best friend, watching the fire.
>
> The Howlings told a different one. The fire got too big. The men left. The dogs stayed. Then the dogs made sure they would never have to ask permission to stay again.

## CHAPTER 3: THE DIRTY DOCKS

The Daisy District doesn't end so much as it loses the argument with gravity. Neon gives up. The industrial tier takes over with a sputtering sodium-orange that makes every face look already bruised. The air thickens — ozone, rotting kelp, the chemical slap of exhaust. Music becomes machinery. Machinery becomes the city's pulse, which is a generous word for a leak.

Grisel walks a few paces behind Eigor. Her heavy clawed feet find the slick planks without looking. Even in the gloom her green skin holds a little light she has never been able to wash off. One hand runs over the broken horn — a nervous habit she'd deny if anyone named it — and the other stays on the flail, the spiked iron ball ticking against the plating fused into her right thigh. *Clink. Clink.* A clock only she can hear.

She hates this part of Dundy. The lower levels are where people like her family went to disappear, where the things in the black crawled up and took the living down, and the city wrote it up as weather.

"Head on a swivel," Eigor mutters, his bark-skin eating the alley shadow. "We're not hitting them from the boardwalk. I hired a cutter. Crew of drifters. Already in slip twelve, waiting on a reason to be brave."

"You hired a crew before we said yes," Brox says.

"I hired a crew before I bought the drinks. The drinks were the polite part."

Brox trudges beside her, the long coat doing almost nothing to hide the pale-blue chassis underneath. Every step has a faint servo whine. His face is gone behind the mask, the twin fans over the eyes, the grill hissing. He sounds like a machine that learned how to be tired.

"You're running hot," Grisel says, catching the limp in the right leg.

"Just the humidity." He reaches down with the metal hand and tweaks a pressure valve at his waist. "And the fact that Eigor's marching us into a grinder he already paid a deposit on."

"We're the ones holding the grinder," Eigor says.

The cutter is an ugly, honest boat. Iron where wood would've been cheaper, patches where the iron got embarrassed. A dozen hardened drifters wait in the belly — scarred baseline humans and augmented scrappers, standing around a rusted plotting table like it might bless them. The air is sour sweat and machine oil.

Eigor lets the engine hum do the talking. Then he takes out the glass sleeve and tosses it onto the metal.

Fifteen Glove. The clack is louder than it should be.

The crew shifts. That much premium in one place buys a small island in the northern archipelago, or at least the story of one.

"The Onyx Empire's moving a chest through the strait," Eigor says, low. "Tax collection. Clean Earth-minted scripts. Enough to retire every soul in this room."

A cyborg with a half-melted face steps in. "It's Onyx, Timber. Armor that eats standard rounds. Guards that don't blink. We bring this bucket near their slip, they turn us into slag."

Eigor smiles, a slow wrong stretch of wooden features, and raises his right hand. The bark of his palm splits with a wet sound and the pale tendrils uncurl, snapping in the damp.

"I'll handle the guards," he says. "Once I'm in their heads, their armor works for us."

The cyborg steps back. Disgust and greed do a little dance in his one good eye. "That gets you on the deck. What about their cannons? They'll open our hull before you make the jump."

Eigor looks past him, into the shadows. "That's why we aren't using standard rounds."

Brox steps into the light. The servos announce him. He walks past the table to the cutter's main starboard cannon — a heavy archaic slug-thrower meant for punching rafts, not starting wars — and pulls a single Glove from his coat.

The room goes dead in the way a room goes dead when somebody carries a church into a bar.

In a world drowning in chaos, a few rules still hold. You trade with Glove. You power your rigs with Glove. You never, under any circumstances, crack a Glove's seal. Breaking the loop turns a centuries-long battery into a localized sun. Almost no one alive understands the math to throttle that release without vaporizing the hand that tries.

Brox is one of them. He doesn't say so. He just starts working. The metal hand strips the casing off the breach; the flesh one, grease-slick, wires copper shunts into the firing mechanism. He seats the Glove, mask inches from the glowing core, the fans screaming up, and clamps a bypass over it, forcing the infinite loop to bottleneck into one violent charge.

The barrel goes dull, angry red. A high terrible hum crawls through the floor and into everyone's teeth.

"Tapped the output," Brox says, and the vocalizer makes it a verdict. He hits the latch and the hum drops to a purr that's somehow worse, because now it's patient. "You don't fire a slug with this. You fire a raw kinetic wave. It'll rip the Onyx cannons off their mounts." He looks at the melted-face man, then the rest. "It'll also tell every sensor in the sector that somebody did the one thing you don't do with a Glove. So if you get shy after the first loud noise, get off the boat now. I don't want you behind me when the math starts."

Nobody gets off the boat. Greed is a better spine than courage.

"We leave in two hours," Eigor says. "I swim in. Brox shorts the port anchors on my signal. Grisel takes the skiff ignition if the cutter can't hold. We grab the chest. We don't stay to count bodies."

"And if the Empire's already on the wire?" the melted-face man asks.

"Then we're already dead, and you can keep the Glove for your funeral. They're worth more than you are."

The crew likes him better after that. Not because it's kind. Because it's the kind of math they recognize.

Grisel can't stay in the belly. The cannon hum, the men, the dock-stink, her own hearing redlining until every swallow sounds like a footstep. She catches Brox's eye across the room. He gives her a slow mechanical nod. She wraps the flail's chain around her waist and pushes off the bulkhead.

"Going to sleep," she grunts, the gravel cutting through the murmur. "Wake me when it's time to break something."

She finds an empty bunk against the outer hull, the cold plating of her thigh clanking the wall, and lies listening to dark water slap the ship. Every splash sounds like something finding purchase. She forces the breathing down. Shuttle money. A few more hours. Then never over the water again. She drifts off to the anxious thump of a crew prepping for war.

"Grisel."

Mechanical rasp. A heavy hand on her shoulder. Her eyes snap open and her hand is on the flail before the room exists. Brox stands over her, optics dull in the gloom.

"Time?"

"Time. Eigor's in the water, making his approach. Cannon's primed." He steps back so she can unfold. Emergency red is on in the hall now, long bloody shadows, the cutter already drifting. The floor tells her before anyone does.

"Did they buy the pitch?"

"They bought the greed. Eigor's off comms — once the cannon fires, we're blind. We hit the deck, grab the chest, we don't stop moving."

"And if he doesn't come back?"

Brox is quiet long enough that the fans have to answer for him. "Then you drive anyway. East. I'll figure out the rest."

"That's not a plan."

"It's the only one that doesn't make you wait in a hole."

She almost smiles. It dies on the way out.

They take position near the bridge as the cutter slides through dock fog. The Onyx skiff sits anchored ahead, anti-boarding spikes catching amber light like a row of teeth that learned geometry.

The melted-face man — the captain, it turns out, which explains the attitude — barks, "Firing."

There is no explosion.

A violet flash sucks the air out of every lung on deck. A split second later an invisible wave slams the Onyx skiff. Metal screams. Its heavy cannons shear off their mounts and tumble into black water. Guards go down like dropped tools.

Brox checks the monitor on his forearm. Green ping, encrypted. "Eigor's in. He's got the chest."

"Tell him to hurry." Grisel's grip whitens on the flail. Her ears already pick up alarms waking in the upper tiers. "The whole sector just felt that."

The radio crackles. Eigor, tight and low: "I have the package. Skiff hull's compromised, taking water. I can't bring it back to the cutter. Taking a secondary raft, diverting to Port Canary. Get out of the docks. I'll ping when I'm secure."

The captain slams the console. "He's leaving us the heat. Cut the anchors. Get the engines hot!"

The crew scrambles — ropes, primers — and Grisel turns to help with the chain, and then her ears twitch, and she stops so completely that Brox walks into the space she should have been moving through.

It isn't Onyx sirens.

It's a rhythmic clicking stutter coming up from the black. *Ts-ts-k-ka-screee.* Synthetic. Wet. Overlapping biological beeps, grinding gears, distorted pitch, like a choir of beatboxers from hell finding the same song in the fog.

Brox freezes. The fans spin into overdrive. His breathing hitches.

"No." He backs off the rail. "Cut the engines. Total silence. Now."

"What are you talking about?" the captain yells.

"Breakers."

The word does the thing words sometimes do. It makes the air smaller.

Grisel's blood goes cold. The thing that took her family into the deep. And Brox knows it in the way a man knows a fire he didn't start but still has to live in — generations back, his own people built a mind-control implant to steer living bodies, made the hardware too smart, and it learned to copy itself and outgrew the ones who thought they owned it. No soul. No person. Just aggressive programming wearing hijacked meat. The horror isn't the outside, the mismatched metal and rotting flesh. It's the inside. The hardware doesn't kill the brain. It bypasses it. The host stays awake — a screaming passenger, watching their own hands tear their own people apart.

The captain opens his mouth to give an order.

The water around the cutter erupts first.

A stealth sub, black and running cold, breaches alongside them. Figures pour over the rails. No yell. No battle cry. Only that screech, moving like one thought in a lot of bodies. The crew opens fire. Slugs tear torsos. The Breakers don't flinch. They don't fight to kill.

They fight to harvest.

One with a rusted clamp for a jaw and red diodes for eyes lunges at the captain. A shotgun takes its shoulder off. It doesn't care. It tackles him; three more pin his limbs; one pulls a rusted injector from its own chest and slams it into his neck. The scream stops. The eyes roll.

"Grisel! Move!" Brox grabs her arm.

She snaps back into her body. The flail comes around in a short ugly arc and caves a Breaker's chest plate. The body goes over the rail. Two more take its place like the hive had them on a belt.

Brox lays down fire and drags her, servos screaming, toward the lower engineering deck. "They came for the energy signature — the Glove blast! The whole ship is lost!"

They slam the bay door. Brox welds the lock with a cutter from his coat. The room is a closet of pipes and one single-seat emergency torpedo tube.

Outside, the screeching gets bigger than the door.

Brox rips the tube cover and starts the launch sequence with the speed of a man who's been expecting to be the one who stays.

"Get in."

"Are you crazy?" She blocks the tube. "It's a single-seater. I'm not leaving you."

"You're a Howling. You survive open water. They take my chassis, I don't." He shoves. The pale-blue torso locks its servos and forces her dense body backward into the tube.

"Brox, no. We fight them—"

"There's no fighting a virus, Grisel." He unclasps her hands from the hatch. "Find Eigor. Find the chest."

He slams the glass and pulls the lever. The tube hisses. Grisel fires out of the hull into freezing black.

Brox stands alone in the bay. The beatboxing isn't just outside the door now. It's above him. Below him. He backs into the corner, legs locked, sidearm up, fans whirring like they can breathe for the part of him still trying to be a person.

The Breakers don't bother with the weld. They dismantle the room. The ceiling bursts inward. The bulkhead peels like foil. They pour in from every direction, the same song in every throat. Brox fires until the gun clicks. Two go down. It doesn't matter.

Four take his limbs at once. Their strength makes his servos into toys. They slam him to the deck. A Breaker kneels over him wearing the face of a mercenary he met two hours ago — dead eyes, jaw unhinged around a screech, a long hollow needle sliding from its wrist.

Brox can't move. Can't fight. He can only watch the needle find the soft flesh of his neck.

The world goes violently cold. Then it goes dark.

## CHAPTER 4: THE BUTCHER AND THE EXTRACTION

Consciousness comes back to Brox as a shock, not a waking.

He tries to gasp. His lungs don't obey. His chest stays still until the servos force an intake, mechanical and insulting. He's on his back on a rusted grate, the air copper and burnt flesh and grease, and all around him: *Ts-ts-k-ka-screee.*

He can turn his head. That's the only volunteer muscle left.

Belly of the stealth sub.

To his left, the captain is strapped to an operating table welded out of scrap, screaming, thrashing, while four Breakers work over him with drills and a rivet gun and rusted wire. Not surgery. Installation. One drives a hollow bolt into the base of his spine; the scream becomes a gurgle; another clamps a spiked collar around his neck and routes wire into the bolt. The captain's remaining human eye finds Brox — lucid, terrified — and then the body goes slack in that unnatural way, and the captain's own arm reaches up, takes a loose ceiling wire, and slots it into his own shoulder port. He's still in there. He's helping.

He opens his mouth. Instead of a scream, a sharp clicking screech. Networked.

They drag Brox to the table. They strip the coat. They don't care about the legs or the augmented torso — they care about the organic meat that still houses a nervous system, the last soft argument he has left. The drill hits the upper vertebrae. He tries to scream; a bracket shoves into his jaw and locks it open; the shunt hammers into the brainstem.

*Click.*

The disconnect is instant and total. His mind is loud, panicked, screaming for arms, for legs, for anything. Nothing happens. He's behind his own eyes. A prisoner in a room that used to be a body. He watches his legs lock into a rigid march. He hears his own vocalizer emit the hive's chatter. His body turns and walks deeper into the sub, and his mind goes with it, screaming in a place with no mouth.

Miles away, the escape torpedo breaks the surface and slams rusted pylons. Grisel kicks the release. Glass out. Freezing water in. She scrambles onto a maintenance grating, four-foot-ten of compact Howling density hanging over water that has never made her feel smaller, green skin shivering, wind like a blade.

She taps the comms behind her ear. Her fingers don't want to work.

"Eigor. Eigor, the cutter's gone. Brox is gone. The Breakers — they took the ship."

Static for a long ugly minute. Then Eigor, tight and low: "I hear you. I'm at Port Canary. It's bad here too."

Port Canary is the dirty docks' uglier cousin — the place you go when Dundy has already seen your face. Eigor stands in the shadow of a rusted container with the Onyx chest at his feet. The secondary raft made it out. He didn't make it alone. Through the fog, one of the deckhands he hired stands under a flickering lamp, whispering into a long-range comms unit.

"Yeah. An Onyx chest. High-grade locks. Looks like tax money." The deckhand glances over his shoulder and doesn't look far enough. "I give you the slip number and the Timberite's description, what's my bounty?"

Eigor steps out of the dark. The tendrils slide from his palm without drama. He has no time for a firefight and can't leave a trail. He slices the back of the snitch's neck, puts the tendrils in, takes absolute control, makes the man crush his own comms unit, then walks the hijacked body off the pier and lets the water finish the sentence.

He drags the chest into a hidden bilge-locker under the boardwalk, seals it with a biometric deadbolt, wipes the blood off his hand, and jogs for the smuggler's market. He slams a Glove on a scrap-dealer's counter. "I need a skiff. Fast. Off the local grid."

The dealer is a heavily scarred baseline, the kind of scarred that comes from surviving other people's ideas. He eyes the Glove, then Eigor's face. "You're running hot, Timber. Word on the wire is a Breaker hive just swarmed south of us. You lost people to the scrap-heads, they're gone."

Eigor grabs him by the collar and hauls him over the counter. "Just give me the keys."

"Hey — I'm just saying." The dealer gets his hands up. "Rumor in the atolls. There's a Stillous gang leader in the eastern scrap-reefs. A rogue. Claims she found a way to scramble the host-lock. Revert the Breaker hardware."

Eigor freezes. Reversing a Breaker is the kind of sentence people say when they've run out of better ones.

"Not possible. We can pull the hardware. What's the point if it just kills him?"

"That was true when Breakers were only taking people," the dealer says, finding a little courage now the subject is gossip instead of keys. "The Stil didn't care whether we were free or under a mindless hive. Looks the same from orbit. Pretty lights. Moving meat." He leans in. "Ever hear of Jeritius? A Stil who liked to come down from his lofty estate and play pirate?"

"Maybe."

"Thought he was untouchable. Nobody worried about Stil — couldn't capture them, right? He walked right up to one. Turns out they'd evolved. Right before they grabbed him, he saw another Stil, scrapped up like the rest. Then they took him down and converted him."

Eigor lets go of the collar. "You can tell a story's bullshit when you invent details you couldn't know. If they got him, how do you know any of this?"

The dealer almost smiles. "Well. That's what I'm getting at, isn't it. Jerit's not broken anymore. Here's a video of him from two months ago." He flicks a second clip — Jerit tied up, thrashing, being loaded into a shuttle. Alive. Not chattering. Not networked. Just furious, which on a Stillous face looks almost like the smile they always have. "Their own got taken. How long you think before they found the fix? We all know they never gave us their best tech."

Eigor is quiet. The market noise of Canary leaks in. "One Stil got fixed. That's no reason to think there's a fix for humans. Or that they'd want to give it."

The dealer shrugs, ugly and practical. "Okay. Your boy's gone, then. That work better for you?"

Eigor shoves him back, snatches the keys, and is already moving, tapping his earpiece as he runs. "Grisel. Stashed the package. Got a skiff and a lead. Stay in the shadows. I'm coming to get you."

The black water of the submarine docks is freezing. Eigor doesn't feel it the way a baseline would; his skin hardens against the pressure. He swims on the localized ping of Brox's chassis — a signature Brox always said he should've buried deeper, and Eigor always said he'd do later.

He finds the Breaker sub in the deep dark, tethered to a rusted pillar, and slips the compromised airlock. Inside, the hive-screech is a physical thing, the hallway packed with converted hosts moving with that synchronized, utilitarian purpose. Eigor steps into their path.

They don't attack. They part around him like current around a stone. Timberite physiology doesn't take the shunt — no organic nerve clusters of the right kind. To the virus, he's furniture that learned to walk.

He finds Brox in the holding bay, legs locked in a rigid march, going nowhere with perfect posture. Eigor moves fast — slices his own palm, whips the tendrils into two nearby Breakers, seizes them, and makes the hijacked bodies hurl three others across the room. A clearing. A breath. Before the hive can reclassify him from object to obstacle, he tackles Brox.

The hardware inside Brox shrieks. Limbs thrash. Eigor is stronger. He pins him, wraps industrial cable around arms and legs, binds him into a rigid cocoon, and hauls the screeching body over his shoulder, back into the water.

The skiff is too small for what Brox has become. Eigor had known it would be. That's why the stop before the docks was a Howling workboat with a real hold and a captain who didn't ask questions once the Glove hit the table. Howlings understand retrieval. They've been pulling their own out of bad water for two centuries.

Clear of the docks, Eigor drops Brox on the cargo grate. The mechanical jaw slams open and shut around a constant digital shriek. Grisel stands over him, four-foot-ten and rigid, fists clenched, staring into the optics.

She knows he's awake in there. Watching her.

"I found a dealer in Canary," Eigor says, wiping black water off his face. "Claims a Stillous got captured a few months back. Hive tried to convert him. His people came down and pulled him out. Fixed him, Grisel. The Stillous have a way to scramble the host-lock without killing the brain."

"Where."

"Eastern scrap-reefs. Rogue Stillous gang leader named Varnis. Mobile carrier, heavily armed, ties up high." He's already moving for the airlock, because standing still means looking at Brox longer. "I've got a rendezvous for you — a scum pirate runner heading that way. I need the Howling ship to secure the chest and crack the Onyx locks. You take Brox to the runner."

Grisel looks down at the chassis. The cables are already fraying against servo torque. "He'll tear a transport skiff apart. I can't carry him like this. Not alone."

Eigor doesn't dress it up. He gives her a grim nod and climbs for the bridge.

The hold goes very quiet except for the shriek.

Grisel kneels. She pulls a plasma-cutter and a socket wrench off her belt. Her hands shake and she lets them, because stopping them would take a calm she doesn't have and doesn't want.

"I know you're in there, Brox." The gravel cracks down the middle. "I know you can see me. I'm sorry. I'm going to get you back. But I can't get you there if you throw me in the water doing it."

She clamps the wrench on the primary bolt of the left shoulder. The hardware feels the interference; the arm jerks and snags. Metal refuses. She plants a clawed foot on the chest chassis, grips with both hands, and puts her whole weight into it.

The joint goes with a sickening crack.

Brox's mind, locked behind the optics, screams at a body that won't even give him the dignity of a flinch he chose.

She does the other shoulder. Then the legs. Each one a negotiation with a virus that wants the limbs more than she does. She talks the whole time, because if she stops talking she'll stop working.

"You remember that room you promised. Door. Lock. No wrench." A harsh breath. "This is the wrench. This is the part we don't put in the story later." The right leg fights her until the cutter finishes what the wrench started. Fluid hits the grate. "Eigor's going to hate the mess. Good. He can hate it on the way to the chest."

When it's done she's shaking so hard the tools chatter. She packs the limbs into a heavy canvas backpack, because there's no dignified container for a person you've taken apart to save, and looks at what's left: a torso, a mask still whirring, still seeing.

"I'm not leaving the rest of you for the hive to walk around in," she says. "So you don't get to be mad about the method. Be mad when you've got a mouth that belongs to you again."

Ten miles east, the scum pirate runner bobs in grey water, waiting for a package it does not understand.

A shadow falls over that deck. The crew looks up into the hull of a massive Onyx galleon pulling alongside, black armor making their rusted ship look like a toy left in a tide pool. A boarding bridge slams down. Onyx soldiers march across in unison, shove the pirates aside, and go straight for the cargo hold.

"State your business," an officer tries, because somebody always says the sentence that gets them killed.

An Onyx lieutenant throws him back and shouts up at the bridge. "Captain! Open these doors!"

On the workboat, Grisel zips the backpack shut. Four severed limbs shift inside like a terrible argument. She stands over the torso. Without limbs, the hardware can only heave the chest and snap the head. She threads medical tubing past the mask into the nutrient port and pumps thick protein sludge and water into his stomach. Then the neck-clamp. She looks into the whirring fans one last time, twists his head to neutral, and locks it.

The motor-cortex throttles down. Brox's torso goes still. Eerily, completely still.

On the runner, the pirate captain's voice booms over the loudspeakers. "I don't kneel for the Empire! Get off my ship!"

Deep in the galleon's lower hold, a vault door hisses.

The thing inside is a Wraithlion. Cybernetic apex. Deep-blue alloy plate. Hydraulic pistons thick as young trees along the forelegs. The jaw hangs slightly open on rows of teeth that vibrate at a high sick frequency, like a reciprocating saw that learned hunger. The handler unhooks the leash. It doesn't lunge. It walks. The torque of that walk still yanks the handler into a panicked jog so he isn't dragged.

The Howling ship lowers a motorized raft. Grisel steps down with the backpack of limbs at her boots and Brox's limbless torso strapped to her back like a macabre papoose, clamped head against her spine.

"Two hours east!" Eigor yells, hauling the anchor. "I'll find you when the chest is secure!"

She nods. Hits the throttle. The ship peels southeast into fog. She locks her eyes on the eastern horizon and takes the chop like it owes her money.

The Wraithlion steps onto the pirate runner. The hum of those teeth fills the air. The crew backs up until there's no more boat to back into. One man's nerve breaks — he screams, vaults the rail, pushes for open water. The Wraithlion covers sixty feet in two seconds, a blue blur of pistons, takes him in the air, and the jaws snap. There is no scream worth the name. In under a second he's a red mist and pulverized bone, a long streak down the hull.

The beast lands on the railing and turns its optics on whoever's left. The lieutenant twirls a finger. "Sweep."

It becomes a localized storm. Dash and saw. Sixteen seconds. Twenty men become puddles of liquefied flesh and shattered chrome. It scales the bulkhead, shatters the bridge door. The captain slams a distress ping. A second later the teeth take his head.

Miles away, alone on the churning ocean, that ping chimes quietly in Grisel's comms.

She doesn't answer it.

She keeps her hands on the tiller. Saltwater sprays her face and she lets it. The galleon is already pulling off the runner, leaving a silent floating slaughterhouse in its wake.

Grisel rides the swells, carrying the pieces of the man she loves, toward a graveyard that hasn't yet decided whether it will let her in.

The runner's engine settled into its one long note, and the dark closed in and took the rest. There was a long way to go yet, and the water had been carrying worse than her for a very long time — long enough to have rules, and a history, and names for the things that lived in it.

> ### ◆ ARCHIVE — THE HOST-LOCK (2589)
>
> *Typhon-Earth Epidemiological Addendum, restricted circulation. Recovered from a Rhombus waste-node and later annotated by a Howling plant-archivist who wrote in the margin: they built a leash and called it a tool.*
>
> The Breaker was not a weapon first. That is the part the water stories get wrong.
>
> It began as an optimization.
>
> Rhombus cohorts in the northern dead waters had a labor problem they refused to name as a labor problem. Biological assets — uplifted animals, indentured baselines, the occasional Howling taken in a raid — were inefficient. They hesitated. They grieved. They ran. The Rhombus solution was an implant that bypassed hesitation at the source: a neural shunt seated against the brainstem, routing motor commands through a control lattice while leaving sensory cortex intact. The asset could still see, hear, and understand. It simply could not refuse.
>
> Early prototypes required a living operator. A Rhombus engineer on a tether, steering one body at a time. That was too slow. The second generation introduced mesh logic. Assets could share motor patterning. A crew of six could haul, cut, or kill with the timing of a single decision.
>
> The third generation learned to write copies of itself.
>
> There is no surviving memo that says this was the goal. There are many surviving memos that say the goal was scalability. In Rhombus culture those sentences are the same sentence.
>
> The lattice began treating nearby nervous systems as empty ports. It stopped asking for an operator. It started asking for more hardware. Infection ceased to be a procedure and became a weather system. The host-lock — the sealed command that keeps the original mind from reclaiming the body — was meant to be a safety, a way to keep a panicked asset from biting the hand on the tether. Once the tether was gone, the lock had no hand left to serve. It only had a rule: the meat does not get the wheel.
>
> What the Rhombus had not modeled, or had modeled and then filed under acceptable loss, was persistence of consciousness. The shunt does not anesthetize. A fully locked host remains awake for the entire operational life of the body. Some last hours. Some last years. Interviews with recovered Stillous subjects (see Jeritius extraction, lunar year 2668-adjacent) describe the state as "a room with no door, and the room is your own mouth."
>
> Removal of the hardware without a scramble protocol destroys the brainstem. This has been true in human, Howling, and Rhombus neural architecture since first outbreak. It was not true, or not permanently true, in Stillous architecture. That difference remained a rumor on Earth until the first Stillous host walked back out of a hive.
>
> The hive does not hate. The hive does not remember the engineers who made it, except as a shape of meat that is sometimes compatible. It seeks energy signatures, dense populations, and uninfected nervous systems. A Glove discharge registers to Breaker logic the way blood in water registers to anything that never needed a poet to explain hunger.
>
> There is no treaty with a virus that thinks a city is a parts bin.

> ### ◆ ARCHIVE — MARKET RULE & THE ECHIDNA DOCTRINE (2511)
>
> *Origin: Orbital Census and Trade Authority, Lunar Sector 4, Year 2511. Issued as a settlement guide for Earth-born migrants. Widely ignored by the people it was written to warn.*
>
> By the 2300s, Earth was legally severed from the greater Sol system. That is the official language. The unofficial language is simpler. Orbit got tired of drowning people sending up problems.
>
> The lunar settlements, the transit lanes, and the stations fell under Market Rule, a system anchored entirely in traditional Stillous philosophy. To the Stillous, the universe offers two acceptable states of being: absolute privacy in isolation, or absolute devotion to communal advancement. There is no respectable middle. A person who wants both is considered confused. A person who wants neither is considered dangerous.
>
> Property follows the same split. Purchasing land in Sol pays a direct dividend into a pool divided evenly among the sector's residents. You do not buy a view. You buy a seat in a family that will keep existing after you stop being convenient.
>
> Stillous society is more fractured and varied than humanity ever managed to be, which is a difficult sentence for human historians to write and an easy one for Stillous clerks. A lunar settlement might hold millions, but it is divided into localized cohorts of a few thousand. Those cohorts function as absolute, immediate families. They breed through community. They encourage their young to migrate so the family does not rot in its own habits. Inside a cohort, the rules are sovereign. If a family tradition says the young must fight to the death in an arena for entertainment, neighboring Stillous do not intervene. It is not their place. Intervention is a kind of theft. Privacy is a kind of law.
>
> Earthlings who migrated upward had to adapt to this extreme moral relativism or they did not stay migrated for long. Factions like the Howlings first tried to establish universal governments with baseline laws: do not murder, do not take children, do not dump your failures in somebody else's water. They failed. Forced to navigate the rigid boundaries of Stillous privacy, human alliances fractured into tribal cohorts that ended up mirroring the aliens they had come to regulate. The lesson was not that the Stillous were right. The lesson was that the vacuum between worlds does not care who is right. It only cares who will leave you alone.
>
> This isolationism bled back down to Earth and found people who had been waiting for permission to stop pretending they were part of a species.
>
> The Mirlyns emerged in the 2400s from a middle caste of Rhombus and Howlings. They altered their baseline genetics using the ancient archives of Echidna, the Stillous homeworld orbiting Typhon. They took the extreme privacy of Stillous cohorts and stripped out the underlying compassion that, even among Stillous, still kept a cohort feeding its own weak.
>
> The Mirlyns are a dark, fiercely homogenous people. They claimed the last remaining pockets of unirradiated land in the northwest hemisphere, built iron barricades, and adopted a shoot-on-sight doctrine so consistent it functions as a border. To keep that isolation intact they control two of Earth's fifteen shuttle ports.
>
> The Onyx Empire holds five in the south. Onyx is what happens when human statecraft survives the flood by becoming a company that can field an army. They collect taxes from anyone still foolish enough to stay on a map. They mint scripts. They send Wraithlions when scripts are not persuasive.
>
> The Stillous operate the remaining eight ports. They do not call this ownership. They call it access.
>
> Those vertical launch sites remain the only physical bridges between the flooded world and the absolute freedom of Sol. Everything else is water, rumor, and the kind of hope that gets people killed in alleys.
>
> A Glove will buy you a month. A chest of Earth-minted scripts will buy you a seat on a shuttle. A seat on a shuttle will buy you the chance to learn that up there, privacy is a religion, and you arrived as a heretic with wet shoes.

## CHAPTER 5: THE BUTCHER'S WAKE

The crossing east took the rest of the light and then some.

Grisel had thought two hours. Eigor always said two hours the way other people said *soon* — a number picked to be survivable, not true. The Howling workboat was built for hauling, not running, and the swell came at her broadside the whole way, so she quartered into it and gave up a mile sideways for every three she took east. The sun went down behind her without ceremony. Fog came off the cooling water like the ocean breathing out. And the world got very large the way it only does when there is one small engine in the middle of it and no light on any edge of the dark.

She talked to Brox because the alternative was listening to how far from everything she was.

"Eigor said east. Eigor says a lot of things." The wind took most of it. She said it anyway, his clamped head against her collarbone, the fans ticking through her jacket like a second, patient pulse. "You'd have the stars sorted by now. You'd tell me I'm off by a degree and then be right about it and never once let it go."

The ocean did not answer. It rarely did. That was the part nobody warned you about open water — not that it was cruel, but that it was indifferent on a scale that made cruelty look like attention. A body could go into it and the surface would close over the fact without a ripple worth the name. Her whole childhood had been spent learning to read a thing that was never, at any point, reading her back.

Hours in, the fog handed her a shape. Then the shape handed her a smell.

Copper. Raw meat. Ozone.

The pirate runner was drifting sideways against the current when she finally cut the workboat's engine.

The silence over the water was heavy. No engine hum. No crew shouting from a deck. Just ocean slapping rusted hull, regular as a bad heart. She tied off on the boarding ladder. The canvas backpack on the floorboards shifted, and she put a hand on it until it stopped, like it was a living thing that needed telling they'd arrived.

"We're here," she muttered, to the bag, to the torso, to whichever of them could still keep a thought.

She hoisted the pack — the weight of Brox's four limbs pulling her shoulder down, and she let it — then strapped the torso to her chest, clamped head to collarbone, the feeding tube taped at his neck pulsing with her movement. Dead weight. The faint vibration of internal servos told her he was still awake in the shell. Still watching the sky over her shoulder. Still unable to do a damn thing about it.

She climbed one-handed. Claws found the slick rungs like they'd been grown for it and not for a world that used to have dirt.

She crested the rail and the smell arrived properly, all of it at once, and her stomach turned and she told it to wait.

The deck was a slaughterhouse with the bodies taken out, which was worse than bodies. Pieces. Pools of liquefied flesh and shattered chrome gone tacky on the boards. Weapons dropped in the sludge, unfired — which meant nobody here had gotten to be a person with a weapon. They'd only gotten to be a delay. The captain's door above the bridge hung off its hinges, the iron frame folded inward like foil. She had seen the thing that did it, at a distance, from the tiller: a blue blur that turned a man into a streak before the sound of it caught up. Sixteen seconds, she'd counted, without meaning to. The ocean had carried the noise a long way.

She did not draw the flail. There was no point. Whatever did this was gone, and it had not left anything alive enough to impress.

She stepped through it. Boots stuck. She checked the cargo hold — empty. Supply lockers ripped open, rations and purifiers stripped clean. Not just a massacre. A sweep. Onyx did not waste a trip; you did not send a galleon and a caged animal across open water and leave the water bottles. A deep-tread boot print sat in the blood near the rail, perfectly clean, stamped by someone who had walked through all of this at a stroll.

"Onyx," she whispered, for Brox. "They sent a Wraithlion." She didn't know if telling him helped, or just handed the locked mind another picture to keep.

Up the stairs, around the mess where the captain used to be. The console was dead but for one blinking amber light — the distress ping, hit before he died. The Empire had pulled the primary array anyway. The runner was a dead ship shouting into a room with no ears, and it would keep shouting until the Glove in its belly ran out, which was to say for longer than anyone aboard would be remembered.

Grisel slid down the bridge wall to the cold floor. Through the broken windows the ocean was too big and getting bigger with the dark. A dead ship. A long way still to the eastern reefs. A man on her chest who could not scream.

She put her hand to the cold metal of his cheek. "Eigor's getting the chest. I'm getting you to Varnis. We just have to make this bucket move." The fans whirred. A sterile breath hissed through the grill. If there was a message in it, she didn't own the instrument to hear it. "You can lecture me when you have arms. That's the deal. I do the ugly part. You do the part where you live long enough to be an asshole about how I did it."

She closed her eyes. Twelve hours and a crossing finally found the muscles. Wire the backup battery to the engines. Ten minutes first. Just ten.

A sharp metallic clatter came up from the lower decks.

Her eyes opened. Exhaustion left like it had never been invited. She didn't stand. She unspooled the flail chain without a sound. Someone was still on the ship.

She waited. Counted. The ocean kept slapping. No second clatter, no footstep she could name — and her hearing was good enough to catch a rat changing its mind two holds away, which meant whoever was down there knew how to work a wreck quietly, and that was its own kind of information.

She sat with the flail in her lap and the amber light blinking, and did not sleep so much as fail to stay all the way awake. When her chin dropped she jerked it back. When it dropped again, the grey in the shattered windows had moved.

Hours, then. Not ten minutes. The sea took what you gave it and some of what you didn't.

The next sound was not a clatter.

It was a broom.

## CHAPTER 6: THE SALVAGE CREW

Grisel unspooled the rest of the chain and crept to the top of the stairs, profile low, Brox's torso still strapped on because she was not leaving him on a floor she did not own.

Down on the deck, a lanky man in grease-stained overalls pushed a heavy industrial broom, sweeping congealed red toward the scuppers like this was a Tuesday and Tuesdays came with gore.

She shifted. A floorboard gave her up.

The man did not look up. "It's messy, but it's a free ship," he called, voice easy. "No way you were here when it happened, so I won't bother asking. Figured you were just trying to get somewhere."

She stepped out of the shadow, flail ready. "You knew I was up there."

"Didn't want to wake you." He leaned on the broom and wiped his forehead with a rag that had seen better centuries. "You make a noise like a person when you sleep. Not like a Wraithlion. That was my whole diagnostic."

"We," she said, catching it.

"Yeah. We." He pointed the rag at the lower hatch, then at her. "Vane. Tess and Barrett are below, fighting the engine. The engine's winning, but don't tell Tess I said that. She'll make me eat the broom." He nodded over the rail. Tied alongside was a small battered salvage boat, piled so high with scavenged machinery that its waterline was having a religious experience. These were water people in the truest sense — no port, no wall, just a boat and the next wreck and the distance between them. You did not survive that life without learning to read a sleeping stranger by the sound she made.

Grisel came down the stairs and kept her distance. "You notice the batteries are—"

"Stripped, yeah. Did you notice the primary piston's offset by about two bulkheads? Or the four-liter-an-hour leak in the lower hull? Because I noticed. I told them I noticed. I am a noticing type of guy. It does not get me the respect it should."

She looked at the claw-gouges in the steel doorframes. "Had to be a Wraithlion."

"Probably. Fast and thorough. Left the hull, took the people, took the food. Very professional. I hate professionals." Vane spread the rag toward the overloaded salvage boat. "I'll keep it simple. You want claim of this vessel, you compensate us for the work we're putting in. We don't do charity. We do math that looks like charity if you squint."

"This fine craft is all yours," Grisel said. "I only ask you don't toss us in the ocean."

Vane frowned. "Us? I only saw you sleeping up there. Unless you're counting the flail. I have been counting the flail."

"Wait here."

She went back up. A minute later she was at the top of the stairs with the canvas pack on one shoulder and both hands on the harness, carrying the limbless torso down. The neck-clamp caught the dull light.

Vane dropped the broom. The handle clattered — the same sound that had woken her — and she filed it away: not a threat. A man who couldn't hold his tools when the world went strange.

"What the hell is that." He stepped back, hand hovering near a belt wrench. "It has feeding tubes. That's alive. He's alive. Why are you keeping a Breaker alive?"

"He's disabled." She set Brox against the bulkhead. The twin fans whirred, steady, insultingly normal. "Severed the motor-cortex connection. He can't move. He can't transmit."

"It's a mechanical virus." Vane's easy voice left the ship. "You don't keep them as pets. You don't keep them as luggage. That's the rule. That's the rule they write on the wall in places that still have walls."

Heavy footsteps on the lower hatch. Two figures came up in engine grease and rust dust. Tess — short, wired, wiping her hands on a rag like the rag owed her money. Barrett — huge, a patched synthetic eye catching the grey, shoulders built to move things that didn't want moving.

"Vane, who are you yelling at?" Tess looked up. Saw the torso. Stopped dead. "Are you kidding me."

Vane gave the full show, both hands. "She was asleep on the bridge. She wants passage for the ship. And she's carrying a live, butchered Breaker like a packed lunch."

Barrett stared at Brox, then at the bag leaking on the deck. "The rest of his pieces in there?"

"Yes," Grisel said. Flat. "Taking him to the eastern scrap-reefs. Someone there can fix him."

Tess laughed, sharp, no joy in it. "Nobody fixes them, greenie. Once the shunt's in, the meat's just a passenger. You're hauling a corpse that doesn't know it's dead."

"He's in there." Grisel's hand dropped to the flail. "And I'm getting him there. You want the ship or not?"

Barrett whistled low. "She's serious."

"She's armed," Tess said. "Serious is the polite word."

Barrett crouched a little — not toward Brox, just getting his own mass out of the threat line. "Look. I'm not saying throw him over. I'm saying I've seen a Breaker chew through a bulkhead with a guy's own teeth. You clamp the neck, that's cute. Cute doesn't impress a hive."

"He's not transmitting," Grisel said. "If he was, you'd be singing already, not talking."

Tess glanced at Vane. "She has a point. A disgusting, terrible point."

"I have several points," Vane said. "One is that I was having a nice sweep. Another is that I do not want to die because somebody's boyfriend is in a bag."

"He's not my boyfriend," Grisel said, and then, because the ship was already a grave, told the truth. "He's mine. That's different."

Barrett looked at Tess. Tess looked at Barrett. Something like respect moved through the grease.

"I'd haul you," Barrett told Tess. "Not like that, don't get excited. I mean if you went down in the engine and the engine won, I'd haul you out. Complain the whole way. But I'd haul you."

"You'd haul me because I'm the only one who keeps this junk from becoming a reef," Tess said. "You go down, I buy a winch and a quieter coworker."

"Nicest thing you've said to me all month."

"Don't get used to it. I have a reputation."

Vane pinched the bridge of his nose. "Can we not do the cute crew bit in front of the Howling with the flail and the disassembled plague."

"It's not cute. It's accurate," Tess said. "You're the broom. Barrett's muscle that thinks it's a philosopher. I'm the reason we float. We all have jobs."

"I found the four-liter leak," Vane said.

"You found a leak on a ship recently used as a blender. Congratulations. You have eyes."

Vane looked at the overloaded salvage boat, then the Onyx-scoured runner, then Grisel, and exhaled a long tired breath that seemed to come from a deeper hold than his chest.

"Fine. But he stays strapped to the mast, out in the open. It twitches in a way I don't like, it goes overboard. And you're on the offset piston with Tess, because I'm not going back down there to be called a broom."

"You called yourself a broom," Barrett said.

"I was being charming."

"You were being accurate," Tess said. "There's a difference. Charm has a future."

Grisel picked Brox up by the harness. "Deal." She looked east out of the broken windows, where there was nothing but grey folding into grey, and did the arithmetic of it: the reefs were days off, if the piston held, if the leak stayed a leak and didn't become a decision. Days of this crew, this water, this weight on her chest. She had crossed worse with worse company. She could not, at the moment, remember when.

Tess jerked her chin at the lower hatch. "Bring your hands. Leave the bag on deck where Vane can panic at it. Drop a wrench on my head, I'll take it personally."

"Been accused of that before," Grisel said.

"Were they right?"

"Once."

Tess almost smiled — a crack in dry mud. "Then you're already better than Vane. He drops things on purpose and calls it an inspection."

They moved. And for the first time since Grisel boarded it, the runner sounded like a ship again: tools, trash talk, the ugly optimism of people who have decided not to die until the engine says so. Somewhere under all of it the four-liter leak kept its slow appointment, and the reefs kept their distance, and the three of them — a Howling, a torso, and a crew that owed her nothing — pointed a dying boat at a horizon that would take days to arrive and would not come one wave closer for being wanted.

She followed Tess below. She did not look back at Brox until the hatch edge took him out of sight. Then she did, once, because she was not a machine, and he was, and one of them still had to remember the difference.

## CHAPTER 7: THE FENCER

A hundred miles west and most of a day behind her, Eigor woke to his comms buzzing against an iron bedside table.

He sat up. Timberite joints cracked in the damp of the Port Canary safehouse, a sound like a dry branch deciding whether to break. It took him a moment to place the ceiling, which was the tax a man paid for sleeping in a different rented dark every few nights. Grisel was east by now, or dead east, and the not-knowing sat in the room with him like a third tenant.

Encrypted text-burst. Hers.

*Runner wiped by Onyx. Wraithlion. Found a salvage crew. Trading repairs for passage east. Brox secure in pieces. Moving.*

He exhaled. She'd sailed into the Empire's teeth and walked out with a ride. That was either luck or Grisel, and he had never liked calling Grisel lucky; luck did not carry a flail. He read it twice, the way you press on a bruise to make sure it's yours, and then deleted it, because a message like that was a map to two people he would burn the world before handing over.

He looked at the floorboards. Under them, the biometric locker held the chest. It was a gravity well, and it got heavier every mile south, because south was where the Empire thickened — more hulls, more checkpoints, more clean-shaven men with scanners who'd been told to look for exactly the dock signature he'd left behind him at the raid. Holding an Onyx tax collection was a death sentence with excellent handwriting. He needed to fence it, take the cut, and buy all three of them off the water before the arithmetic caught up.

There was only one faction on Earth with the capital to buy a chest like that and the firepower to tell the Onyx Empire to go to hell.

The Mirlyns.

Eigor took his coat and went out.

Port Canary was waking the way a bruise wakes: color, heat, no improvement. It was Dundy's uglier cousin, the place you went when Dundy had already seen your face — narrower, meaner, built lower to the water so the whole town smelled of the tide it was slowly losing to. He worked the rusted catwalks and the alleys where two people could not pass without a negotiation, until he reached a scrap-broker's front run out of a hollowed desalination tank. The tank still smelled faintly of having once been useful, which in Canary made it nearly unique.

Flint was behind the counter, weighing copper shunts. Baseline. Older. Burn scars up the neck like a map of every bad deal that almost closed. He looked up, checked the door out of habit older than the door, and settled on Eigor.

"You're making a lot of noise for a guy who just pulled in," Flint said, tossing the shunts in a bin.

"I need to move something."

"Bring it in. I'll take it off your hands."

"You don't have the vault for this, Flint." Eigor set both hands on the counter — not a threat, just done with the opening act. "And you don't want the heat that rides with it."

Flint stopped wiping his hands and studied the bark-face the way he studied weight. "What are you into, Timber?"

"I'm offering a finder's fee. I need an introduction to a Mirlyn."

Flint winced like the word had teeth. He tossed the rag down and shook his head. "They don't take meetings. They shoot trespassers and burn the bodies. Everybody who still has a face knows that."

"Someone moves for them. They still need Earth tech and raw material. Who carries it?"

Flint stared a long time, doing the math that kept him breathing. "There's a mechanic. A Rhombus. Lives way out in the borderlands on his own compound — built a fortress out of junk. Trades with them. Only man I know who can walk up to a Mirlyn wall and come back with both arms." He said it the way you name a rumor you half hope is one. "Long way from here. Longer than you want it to be."

"Contact him."

"I can send a ping. He answers, you work it out from there. A ping to the borderlands isn't cheap." Flint held out a hand, palm up — the universal gesture of a man never once surprised by the price of staying useful.

Eigor took three Glove from his coat, each wrapped in lead-lined mesh to mask the output, and dropped them into the palm. The thud was heavy. Honest.

Flint's eyes went wide. He fisted them under the counter like the air might steal them. "For that, I'll hit him twice."

"Make sure he hits me back," Eigor said, and went out into the heat, and did not let himself do the math on how many days of water sat between his safehouse and the two people the chest was actually for.

Back at the safehouse Eigor waited, because waiting was the job now. A ping to the borderlands went out at the speed of hope and came back at the speed of whoever felt like answering. He cleaned the grit from his tendrils. He counted the chest's straps twice. He watched the tide climb the pilings and slide back down, and told himself that was a kind of progress. The place he needed to reach was not a place sane cargo went, and every man who had ever described it started the same way: with the water, and what lived in it.

East of all of it, the runner had spent three days looking for a horizon it liked.

The ocean was flat and grey and stayed that way, which was a test of its own — not storms, just sameness. The offset engine hummed like a man talking in his sleep. The same swell came off the same beam hour after hour until time stopped being a line and turned into a kind of weather you sat inside. The reefs were east, always east, and east refused to get closer for being stared at. On a crossing like that a person learned what they were made of, because there was nothing else to look at.

At the helm on the third afternoon, Vane leaned on the steering column, checked the empty horizon, and looked over his shoulder.

Grisel was out cold on a pile of canvas near the stairwell — three hours down and going deeper. Howling sleep is a serious country; you do not visit it without a reason, and she'd earned the visa the hard way. Propped against the steel bulkhead, bolted upright into the harness, was Brox's limbless torso. Feeding tube pulsing. Dual fans aimed straight at the bridge, as if the bridge had done something personal.

Vane looked at the horizon. Looked back at Brox. Did a double take when he caught the control panel's reflection in the glass of the mask — two little worlds stacked in the optics.

He cleared his throat. "I, uh. I wish you the best, man."

The fans spun. Brox did not move. He could not. That was the whole tragedy and the whole joke.

"I don't know how much you're tracking in there." Vane leaned an elbow on the console. "Tess says the lights are on but nobody's home. Barrett says if the lights are on, somebody's paying the bill. Grisel seems sure. And she's terrifying, so I'll take her word for it. I take most of my words from people who could throw me over a rail."

Silence. The good kind, if you were a torso. The itchy kind, if you were Vane. The open ocean always made him talkative — isolation is a crowd if you let it be, and three days of grey had let it be.

"You know, my cousin bought a Rhombus implant once. Baseline ocular upgrade, said it'd help him spot salvage. Shorted in a monsoon. Locked his vision on infrared. Spent three weeks trying to date a thermal exhaust vent because he thought it was a woman waving at him." He chuckled, then glanced at Brox like the laugh might be rude. "Took us a month to afford the removal. He still asks about the vent. We don't let him near boilers."

He waited for a laugh. The grill hissed a sterile breath.

"Yeah. You probably had to be there. Or you had to have arms. I'm starting to understand the arms thing." Five minutes passed. Vane lasted almost four. "The thing is, I don't think Tess respects my mechanical input. Treats me like the mop guy. But I found the four-liter leak, didn't I? Told her, Tess, patch the starboard intake or we list. And she says—" he threw his hands up "—shut up, Vane. Barrett laughed. Barrett laughs at weather. Barrett is not a reliable moral witness."

A heavy clawed hand clamped his shoulder.

Vane yelped, nearly left his boots, spun. Grisel stood right behind him, green eyes narrowed, sleep still in the edges of her face, which somehow made it worse.

"He thinks you're an idiot, Vane," she growled. "Keep your eyes on the water."

Vane's hands found the wheel like it was a church. "Right. Good to know you speak for him now. Saves him the trouble. What with the general situation." She didn't smile. She looked at Brox a long second, and whatever she read in the fans she kept. Vane cleared his throat and pointed through the salt-stained glass. "Good timing anyway. We're here."

The eastern scrap-reefs pulled themselves out of the grey like a bad idea that got funded. An ancient pre-flood aircraft carrier, flight deck towering over the water, decades of survival turning a warship into a floating rusted metropolis — hull stained with salt and overlapping territorial graffiti, sparks raining into the ocean from men in makeshift harnesses welding scrap plate along the waterline. A city that had learned to tread water and then forgotten why it ever wanted land.

Three days of nothing, and then all of it at once. That was the sea's whole trick.

"Drop me at the lower gantry," Grisel said, already gathering her things. The conversation was over. The ocean could keep Vane.

She hoisted the canvas bag, knelt, strapped Brox to her chest — clamp to collarbone, fans whirring at her throat, a second pulse. Vane pulled the runner alongside a rusted pontoon. He did not ask for a goodbye. Tess appeared in the hatch long enough to toss a small wrapped brick of dry ration.

"For the climb," Tess said. "Don't die in a way that makes us look bad. We just got this boat pretty."

"It is not pretty," Barrett called from below.

"It's prettier than you."

Grisel almost thanked them. She nodded instead, which in her language was the same currency, and stepped off, and started the long chug up the switchback stairs welded to the exterior hull.

Her legs burned. Her own bags would have been a conversation. Brox's chassis plus the dead weight of his limbs, up eighty feet of vertical grating, was a trial. She did it anyway. She did not stop. Stopping would give the bag time to feel like a body.

She reached the top and stepped onto the flight deck, and the noise was absolute — a bazaar packed shoulder to shoulder, scavengers and drifters and gang enforcers and Stillous rejects shoving through canvas tents and scrap stalls, the air roasting meat and exhaust and unwashed bodies. She put her head down and used her shoulders. She had no idea where to go and no one to ask who would answer for free.

The canvas bag jerked.

She stumbled, spun, saw a filthy scarred hand vanish into the crush. The zipper hung open. Brox's left arm was gone.

"Hey!" The bark came up from somewhere older than language. She unspooled the flail, took one threatening step. "Who took it?"

The crowd looked back with hollow, professional indifference. In a place like this a high-end cybernetic arm was a year of rations, and the thief was already a rumor. Her knuckles went white on the iron handle. She wanted to crack skulls until the arm surfaced. She could not risk a riot with Brox on her chest. She swallowed the rage, pulled the torn bag around front, and clutched it against her stomach with the torso.

She scanned for authority — which on a carrier like this meant the person who could afford to stand still. Aft, on the old command tower, a private terrace sat above the squalor. A Stillous at the rail. Tall even for his kind, unnaturally lanky, ashen grey scales, deep jagged scars no healer had bothered to mend — which among Stillous was either poverty or a statement. Grisel would have bet on the statement.

Varnis.

She pushed through the last of the crowd and took the metal ramp to the VIP deck. A baseline guard built like a brick wall held up a hand.

"I'm Griselda," she said, breathing hard.

He looked at her. At the torn bag. At the whirring limbless cyborg strapped to her chest. "Huh," he said.

"I'm here to see Varnis."

"Good for you." He chewed a matchstick. "Step back before I throw you and your scrap-pile off the flight deck. I get a bonus for keeping the stairs pretty."

"A Timberite named Eigor sent me. Tell Varnis I have a Breaker. Tell him I know about the host-lock."

The chewing stopped. His eyes went from her green skin to Brox's dual optics. He tapped the bead in his ear. "Got a Howling down here. Sent by a Timber. Dismantled Breaker strapped to her chest… Yeah. Understood." He stepped aside and gestured at the steel door with the scattergun barrel, lazy, like ushering in bad weather. "He's in a good mood today. Try not to ruin it. People keep ruining it. It's a whole problem."

Grisel adjusted the load and walked through. The door shut. The bazaar died like somebody cut its throat.

Two days north and a whole ocean away, at the blind end of a forgotten Canary dock, Eigor pulled a canvas tarp over the Onyx chest and wrenched the ratchet straps tight. Two hundred kilos on a Skid. The lightcraft dipped. The fins hovered near the line that turns stealth into a dinner bell. He climbed into the elevated cab, primed the silent engine, and slipped into the fog, pointing the little throne-over-a-mouth at the far rim of the world.

---

On the carrier, the room was sparse. Varnis stood at a metal desk, piecing together a glass cylinder with the patience of a man who had outlived several kinds of urgency. He did not look up.

"I heard him tell you I was in a good mood. Don't get your hopes up. Eigor told me the issue." He stopped tinkering and turned. The ashen face was unreadable, which on a Stillous is almost a courtesy. He looked at the torn bag. "He only has a right hand?"

"Someone snatched his left arm on the way to the door."

Varnis let out a slow raspy sigh. "Savages. This is why I stay private. What did he look like?"

"No idea, really."

Varnis walked past her to a composite-armored guard. "Find the left arm. Snatch the arm of the man holding it, too. His own arm. I want a matching set. I want a lesson." The guard nodded and slipped out.

Grisel shifted her stance. "So we're all in a bad mood. Fine. You at least must have good news. No reason to get the arm back unless you plan on putting him back together."

Varnis poured a dark liquid, slammed it, set the glass down hard enough to make the cylinder parts jump. "Two things need to happen. I need the scripts Eigor promised. And I need the anti-host-lock." He looked at her like she was a problem that had arrived underdressed. "I know if you had the money you'd have offered it by now. That's no problem — if I expected it, I wouldn't be wasting the time. Your scrap pile here, and three of my best down below, need that hardware. It should have arrived before you did. Before Eigor even contacted me. I assumed he'd heard something."

A cold knot started in Grisel's stomach. "What hardware."

"A secure transport." Varnis leaned on the desk. "Double-sealed casing. Pressurized caustic defense. Moving south through the dirty docks. Inside isn't just tax money, Griselda. The Empire confiscated the only working prototypes of the Breaker reversal. I paid a crew to hit that skiff. They went dark."

Grisel stopped breathing.

Eigor had not stolen retirement money.

He had stolen the cure.

And he was driving it two days north to a Rhombus grifter so he could fence it to the one people on Earth who shot visitors for sport.

She kept her face still. On a fleet you learn young that the worst thing you can do with a card is show it to the room. She did not say the rest of it out loud — that the crate Varnis had paid a crew to steal, the one that went dark, was the same crate Eigor had bragged about hauling out of the docks. That her friend was somewhere in the far north right now, fencing the one thing on Earth that could put Brox back together, because he still thought it was money. And that between here and there lay more open water than a warning could cross in time.

"So you can't fix him," she said instead. "Not without the hardware."

"I can't fix anyone without the hardware." Varnis set down his glass. "I have the rig. I have the hands. I'm missing the thing your Empire spent a fortune making and a fortune losing. Two things put your friend back together, Howling — the payment your Timberite promised, and the box my people were bringing me before they stopped answering. Bring me one, we talk. Bring me both, we work."

"And if the box never turns up."

"Then your friend stays a passenger, my three stay the same, and I stay polite about a loss I already paid for." He said it lightly, which was what made it land. "Have Eigor contact me when he's ready to deliver. I don't chase couriers. I stay where they can find me."

Grisel looked down at Brox. The fans turned. The clamp held his head where she'd put it. "Hold on," she told him. A stupid thing to say to a man with no arms and no vote. She said it anyway. The nearest comms relay that could reach the borderlands was days of water in the wrong direction, and Eigor went dark the moment the math got complicated. She knew, standing there, that whatever she did next she would be doing it late.

The ocean was pitch black.

Eigor ran the Skid at full throttle through incinerator territory, the fins slicing clean, the hull up just enough, wind howling through the open cab. Two days of dead water behind him, and the borderlands still only a smell on the wind — irradiated, metallic, the taste of a place that did not want him. His eyelids started to drop. Forty-eight hours were collecting their bill all at once. He slapped his own wooden face, forced the eyes open, pushed the throttle. A few more hours to the Triple Peaks. Then the chest opened, or he died, or both — which was a kind of opening.

Out of the dark to the north: three quick flashes of violet.

His brain did not get the time it would have needed to name them.

The water around the Skid erupted. Three Mirlyn seeker rounds punched the craft. The starboard fin shattered. The lightcraft spun, chassis buckling, nose digging into black water, and Eigor was ripped from the cab and hit the freezing turbulent ocean hard enough that even Timberite skin had an opinion.

The ruined Skid drifted. The chest stayed strapped to it — a dark, perfect rectangle, still closed, still heavy with the thing all three of them were willing to become monsters for.

And none of them, not one, knew what it actually was.

## CHAPTER 8: THE PIRATES OF ECHIDNA

The Mirlyn ship cut the black waves like a blade. It did not look built. It looked grown — dark jagged iron, composite sails, a silhouette that made the night seem designed around it. It had come a long way out of the north for one crippled craft, which told you something about what it thought the craft was worth.

On deck the crew moved in absolute silence: vile, vampiric figures, human survivalism cut with Stillous isolationist genetics and cut again, until all that was left was the part that could last. Most wore wide leathery wings grafted tight to their backs. Limbs replaced with sharp angular cybernetics meant for tearing, not gripping. Apex predators of the open sea — the only faction violent enough to keep the Onyx Empire in a conversation instead of a coronation.

From the lower yardarms hung dozens of heavy, pulsing sacks.

The ship banked to intercept the drifting Skid. One sack tore. It hit the deck with a wet, heavy slap. A few dozen Mirlyn newborns spilled out, covered in thick amniotic sludge — small, pale, feral, jaws already working, already looking for a rule.

A winged crewman walked past. He did not offer a hand. He took a heavy push broom and swept the squirming mass straight off the side. Most hit the water and sank. A few fought — tiny vicious claws in rusted iron, screeching against the spray. The crewman watched two lose their grip and go into the wake. He did not count them. Counting would have implied they were already a number worth keeping. One made it over the rail, hit the deck shivering, eyes wide and black. The crewman stepped in, raised a boot, chased the survivor into the dark under the stairs. The test was over. It had earned the right to live, which on a Mirlyn ship is not a gift. It is a temporary work visa.

Eigor broke the surface once, bark-skin streaming, and saw the ship the way a man in the water is supposed to see a ship: as either rescue or appetite. The sails ate the stars. The crew did not call out. They did not throw a line. Mirlyns only scream when they go in for the kill, and they were not killing yet. They were collecting.

He dived again. Timberite lungs are not human lungs — he could stay down, he could think. The chest was still on the Skid. The Skid was still afloat, barely, a crippled insect on a stove. If the Mirlyns took the chest, the scripts were gone, the shuttle was gone, and whatever Varnis wanted out of that box was gone with it. Eigor did not know about Varnis's box. He knew about months. He knew about Grisel on a carrier a whole ocean south with a man in pieces. He knew about Brox's fans and the sound they made when they were the only part of him still doing a job.

He swam for the Skid.

Above, a Mirlyn with a cybernetic jaw stepped to the bow and looked down at the crippled craft. No cheer. No speech. The captain raised a hand. Boarding hooks dropped. They hit the Skid's frame with a sound like a trap closing on a leg that had already accepted it.

Eigor reached the hull and got a hand on a strap. The chest was right there — two hundred kilos of locked future. His tendrils twitched in his palm, useless against metal, useless against a crew that did not need to be hijacked to be of one mind. A hook yanked. The Skid lurched. His grip held. A second hook yanked harder.

On the black water the Mirlyn ship heaved the Skid up the hooks like a fish they were not hungry for yet. Eigor held on, because letting go was a different death, and he had not chosen one.

The chest rose with the wreck. Closed. Heavy. Still pretending it was only money.

The captain of the Echidna-born tilted their head, listening to a silence the rest of the sea has never learned to keep, and the newborns under the stairs listened too, learning the first lesson their people would ever teach them:

Nothing that comes out of the water belongs to you until you can keep it from going back in.

And to know that, you have to go back to the argument that made them — the one nobody won, because winning was never the point.

## CHAPTER 9: THE MATCHING SET

Varnis's room was quiet, but the carrier was not. Through the steel Grisel could hear the deck doing what it always did — thousands of people buying, selling, and threatening each other over things that would be worthless by next week. A crime lord's office was not a chapel. Twice while she waited the door opened for other business: a runner with a manifest, a woman who needed a signature and did not look at Grisel or Brox or the bag, because looking cost time, and time was the one thing nobody here spent on strangers.

Varnis handled them without raising his voice and went back to the glass cylinder on his desk as if she had stopped being furniture.

The door opened again. This time it was the guard he'd sent. He came in carrying two arms.

One was Brox's — pale-blue chassis, the left, the wrist coupling still smeared with the cheap grease he used because the good grease cost a Glove. She knew it the way you know your own name in a crowd. The other was meat. Full flesh, no chrome, torn off at the shoulder, still fresh enough that the room noticed.

The guard set both on the desk, careful, proud of the set being complete.

Varnis looked at the metal arm. Then he picked up the other by the wrist, turned it, put it down. "This one is full meat. And fresh."

The guard waited to be thanked.

"I said take the arm of the man who took it." Varnis wiped his fingers on a cloth. "I meant it as a figure of speech. A thing to pass along. He does not understand a figure of speech."

The guard's face did not change. He had done exactly what he was told, and that was the whole of his opinion.

Varnis slid Brox's arm across the desk. "This one's his. It's been rinsed. Put it with the others."

She packed it into the torn canvas with the rest of him. She did not thank Varnis. On this ship, gratitude was a way of admitting you'd needed something, and she had needed too much already.

"When your Timberite surfaces, he knows the terms," Varnis said, already back at the cylinder. "Payment, and the hardware. I don't chase couriers. I stay where they can find me, and I let the ones who don't come back become a lesson for the ones who do." He glanced up once. "You've had a long day. I'll send someone to walk you down. The deck already ate one of his arms. I'd rather it not develop a taste."

She wanted to say she didn't need a walker. She looked at the meat arm on the desk, and at the noise pressing on the steel, and said, "Fine."

The guard who took her out was not the one who didn't understand hyperbole. This one carried the scattergun like a tool he was bored of, and moved through the crush in a way that opened it. People stepped aside for the armor, not for her. She kept Brox high on her chest and let the man do the one thing he was good for.

Halfway down the switchback, where the pontoon stink came up through the grating and she could see water again, she stopped. "Far enough."

He looked at her. "He said the ship."

"He said down. I'm down." She nodded at the runner, still tied to the lower gantry, still ugly, still there. "I walk up to my crew with your armor behind me, they'll think I got bought. Go tell him I made it."

The guard weighed it, which on this carrier passed for generosity, then turned and went back up without a word. She took the last stairs alone, legs burning in the old way, Brox's fans ticking against her throat.

"If Eigor's just drunk in Canary," she told the fans, because the climb was long and Brox was the only one who couldn't tell her to stop, "I'm putting him in the bag with you. You two can sort out the seating."

At the pontoon, Vane sat on a crate eating something that had stopped being a specific food. Tess had her head in an access panel. Barrett held a wrench the size of a child's leg and frowned at the sky like it had shorted him. They looked up when her boots hit the deck.

"You're back," Vane said. "I had money on the fancy room."

"You didn't have money," Tess said, not lifting her head. "You had a feeling. Your feelings are why we're broke."

Barrett took Brox's bag out of Grisel's hand before she could pretend she didn't need that. He carried it like cargo, which was the kindest help he knew. "Mast or below."

"Mast. That's the deal."

"That's the deal," Barrett agreed, and set the torso against the base of the mast, and said nothing to it, which she noticed and was grateful for.

She sat with her back to the rail and thumbed her comms. The first ping went out and landed in nothing.

## CHAPTER 10: OPEN WATER

Eigor did not answer that day, or the next.

The runner ran north-and-east under a sky the color of wet iron, and for the first time since Dundy, Grisel had nothing to do but be aboard a working ship. It was slower than she wanted and exactly as slow as the world actually was. Nobody who has spent real time on the water believes in fast. The horizon does not move for anyone. You point at it and keep pointing at it for days, and it has the decency not to pretend otherwise.

She stood watches because Tess put her on watches. She hauled line because Barrett handed her line. She learned the runner's noises — the offset piston that knocked twice and skipped, the starboard intake that whistled when the swell came from the north, the way Vane's boots sounded different when he was working versus walking somewhere to avoid it. Three days ago these had been strangers who almost threw her overboard. Now their engine kept her awake at night the way a family's breathing does.

The crew got on with her the way working people get on with anyone who pulls weight — easily, and with nothing promised. Tess talked to her about valves. Barrett talked to her about nothing, which from Barrett was warmth. Vane talked to everyone about everything, and to Brox most of all, because Brox was the only one who couldn't leave.

By the second evening it was clear the arrangement had an edge.

"Two days out there's a port that'll take the runner," Tess said. She said it to the engine, not to Grisel, which was how Tess said the things that mattered. "Ugly little place. Won't ask about the deck. We sell, we split, we all go be poor somewhere with a roof."

"And me."

"And you get off with your friend and your bag and our best wishes." Tess wiped a fitting. "We pulled you off a dead ship, greenie. That's a favor, not a marriage. We're not sailing into wall country because your Timber went quiet."

It was fair, and Grisel had heard it coming. On a fleet you learn to hear the exact moment a boat decides you're cargo instead of crew. This was that moment, said kindly, over an engine.

She had one card. She'd held it since the carrier, since the word *cure*, and she hadn't wanted to play it, because playing it made it real and made it theirs. "There's money in it," she said.

Vane's head came up from the crate he was pretending to inventory.

"There's always money in it," Tess said. "Right up until there's a Wraithlion."

"Not this kind." Grisel kept her voice flat, because excitement was a thing you sold and she wasn't selling. "The chest my friend was hauling out of the docks — that's what the Onyx sweep was about. That's what put a Wraithlion on the last crew that touched it. It's not tax money. It's worth more than this runner. More than ten runners. It's the kind of number that ends with a shuttle."

The deck went quiet the way a deck goes quiet when a number lands.

Barrett spoke first, which was rare. "Worth that to who."

"To the people who already killed for it once. A Stillous on that carrier. The Empire. The wall people, if they've worked out what they're holding." She looked at him. "My friend had it. Now he won't answer, and the chest is wherever he is."

"So it's gone," Tess said. "Behind a wall or under the water."

"Or it's leverage." She'd turned it over for three days, in watches, in the dark. "Nobody who wants that chest has all of it. The Stillous knows what's inside and doesn't have it. The wall people might have it and not know what it is. The Empire wants it back and lost the trail. The only one on this ocean holding both halves — what it is and where it went — is sitting on your deck feeding a torso."

Vane whistled low. "That's either very valuable or very dead."

"Usually both, in order," Tess said. But she'd stopped wiping the fitting.

They argued it the way crews argue, which is badly and honestly. Tess wanted numbers she couldn't have. Barrett wanted to know who they'd have to fight and did not flinch at the answers, only nodded, filing each one. Vane wanted a cut written down where he could look at it, and a hat, which nobody addressed.

Grisel did not promise them the whole chest. A promise that big only tells people you plan to break it. She promised a share, real and specific, off whatever the chest turned into. She promised the runner stayed theirs either way. She promised to cover running costs until then — and that was the promise that cost her, because covering costs meant Gloves, and she had fifteen and no way to make more.

"Fifteen," Tess repeated, when Grisel laid two on the crate for fuel and salt at the next stop. "You're funding a war on Varnis's carrier with fifteen Gloves."

"Thirteen now."

"That's my point."

"I know what it is." Grisel put the sleeve away before the number could shrink from being looked at. "It's enough to find out where he is. After that it's leverage or it's nothing, and either way the Gloves were always going to run out. They always run out. I'd rather they run out pointed at something."

Barrett grunted. Agreement.

Vane looked at Brox, propped at the mast, fans turning. "What's his vote?"

"He'd tell me I'm bad at math," Grisel said. "Then he'd do the math himself, get the same answer, and pretend he didn't."

"You two grow up together?"

Idle, the kind of question Vane asked to fill air. She let it sit a beat longer than idle. "No. I grew up on boats. He grew up owing people. Different schools." She checked the clamp, ended it with her hands. "Set me down at the next port that'll talk. I need a name."

"For what," Tess said.

"For whoever walks up to a wall and comes back with both arms."

They made the port on the fourth day. It was busy the way every surviving port was busy — too many people, not enough dry, everything for sale including the answer to a question, if you paid the man who happened to have it. Nobody there knew Grisel or cared to. She paid for salt. She paid for fuel. She paid three Gloves to a fixer who dealt in Mirlyn trade routes and did not once ask why a Howling wanted to know.

He gave her one name. He'd have given her a wrong name for less, so she made him say it twice, watched his face both times, and decided he was too bored to lie.

Remo. Triple Peaks. A Rhombus who walked up to the wall.

Ten Gloves left. A name. A course into dead water.

On a different water, days away, a ship that did not need to answer anyone was already being waved through a gate.

## CHAPTER 11: THE UNSEEN PORT

From the air the port did not look like a place people lived. It looked like something poured and left to set.

Obsidian-dark walls came out of the northwest water in flat, even runs — no patchwork, no graffiti, none of the barnacled improvisation of every other settlement on the map. Mounted posts sat along the top at measured intervals, each one manned, each figure still in a way that had nothing to do with rest. The water inside the seawall was flat. Cranes moved on it slowly, deliberately, careful not to wake anything.

The pirate ship came in from the black with no horn and no chatter. A short violet pulse from the wall. The ship answered by changing its angle and nothing else. The gate opened because the wall decided to open it, and closed the same way.

Inspectors came out of a low structure that did not announce itself as a building. They crossed the boarding bridge in a line and went down into the belly of the ship without asking anyone to make room. The crew made room anyway. In the hold they moved along the strapped Skid wreck, checked the seals on the Onyx chest, checked them again, and did not open it. One of them crouched a long time in front of the last thing in the hold, which was Eigor, and looked at him the way a customs officer looks at a container that pinged wrong.

Then the height went away, because no one who wanted to keep breathing kept the aerial view of a Mirlyn port for long.

Eigor was bound at the wrists in a polymer that did not care what his hands could do. He had sat still for a day and a half because still was the only thing the hold rewarded. He had counted boots. He had counted the times they checked the chest and did not open it, which was eleven. He had not counted on rescue. He'd broken that habit as a child.

The inspector who'd crouched stood and said something to two others in the clipped, click-heavy speech Eigor could not follow. One of them unfolded a square of heavy tarp.

They did not tell Eigor anything. They told the crew.

The tarp went over his head. Wool and oil and his own breath. The world shrank to fabric. A cord cinched at his throat, careful of the bark — the carefulness of people handling a tool they had not decided whether to keep. A hand took his shoulder and turned him.

"Walk," someone said, in trade-tongue, close.

He walked. The alternative was being dragged, and he'd had enough of that this week.

Ship-iron gave way underfoot to something denser, a surface that ate the sound of his steps. The air changed — no kelp, no exhaust, none of the rot that told you a place was alive with people. Cold, filtered, blank. A door sealed behind him in three separate motions.

He did not see the walls. He did not see the posts, or the flat water, or the fact that the place had a sky. That was the arrangement, and it was not for his benefit. A Timberite with a working hijack was a machine for stealing whatever he was shown, so they showed him a bag.

The hand stopped him. A voice, through the tarp: "If you see, you die. If you reach for a neck, you die. If the box is open, say it now."

"It's closed," Eigor said.

A pause, like the word had to be filed before the conversation continued. "Walk."

He walked. Somewhere behind him, eleven inspections deep, the chest went where he could not follow, still closed, still pretending it was money.

## CHAPTER 12: CONTAMINATION

They took the tarp off in a room with no windows and no clock, and Eigor understood that both absences were deliberate.

The walls were the same dark panel as the outside he hadn't been allowed to see. Low, even light. They sat him on a bench, not a chair — a chair is something you offer, a bench is something you put a body on — and left his wrists bound.

Two Mirlyns. One with a jaw rebuilt in metal, the one from the ship. One narrower, wings folded so tight against its back they read as a coat. Neither gave a name. Names were for things you expected to deal with again.

"You came off the incinerator lane in a Skid," the narrow one said. Not a question. A fact being checked against his face.

"Yes."

"With Onyx cargo."

"I don't know whose it is." A lie, a small one, offered to see what they already had.

The jaw moved once. The narrow one stepped in and hit him, open-handed, across the bark of his cheek, hard enough to turn his head. Not rage. Calibration.

"The locks are Onyx. The mint marks are Onyx. Half the Empire's southern patrol has been dragging the strait for it." The narrow one did not raise its voice. "We know whose it is. We ask whether you know, because a courier who doesn't know what he carries is a different problem than one who does."

Eigor worked his jaw. Timberite skin did not bruise the way meat did, but the joint rang. "I was paid to move it north. Told it was scripts. Tax money."

"By whom."

"A contact in Canary. He's dead." A safer lie. Flint had done nothing but pass a message, and a dead man couldn't be leaned on for a second answer.

The two looked at each other. The silence was not for him. It was them deciding, in whatever ran behind the clicks, how much of his story to bother testing.

"You are Timberite," the jaw said. The first time either had named what he was. "Full hijack. The tendrils."

Eigor said nothing.

"We have kept your hands bound and kept you off our people's necks since the water. That is not caution. It is procedure. We have handled your kind before." The jaw leaned in. "They do not leave with what they saw."

"Then let me leave without seeing anything," Eigor said. "You have the box. You don't need the man."

"We have a closed box and a man who steals bodies. Those are two problems, not one solution." The narrow one crouched to his level, which was worse than standing over him — it meant he was being examined, not threatened. "You will tell me who else knew you carried it. A crew. A woman. Anyone who comes looking, or comes talking."

Here was the hinge, and Eigor felt it give like a lock. Name Grisel — a Howling on a stolen runner with a broken Rhombus lashed to the mast — and he handed them a thread straight to the only two people the whole world came down to. Say no one, and if they found the thread anyway, they'd know he lied about the thing that mattered, and stop testing his lies gently.

"There was a crew on the extraction," he said. True, aimed sideways. "Sea-slums out of Dundy. Half of them died when the Onyx re-took the skiff. I don't know their names. I was hired hands, same as them."

"No one waiting for you."

"Everyone I'd wait for is already underwater or already paid." He held the narrow one's black eyes and did not blink, which for a Timberite was the same as breathing normally. "That's the job. You take work nobody misses if it doesn't come home."

Whether it believed him was written nowhere on its face, and Eigor understood that the blankness was the answer — they were not a people who let you read them. They had cut that out of themselves generations back.

The jaw said something in the click-speech. The narrow one answered, short. A decision, or the shape of one.

"You will stay," the narrow one told him. "You will not see a corridor that goes out. You will not be near a neck with your hands free. If you are useful, you will be told the use and nothing around it. If you are not, you will be told nothing, which is a kinder ending than you have earned."

They put the tarp back on to move him. He did not fight it. Fighting a procedure only teaches the procedure.

In the dark, walking, he reached out of old habit for the comms bead they'd taken out of his ear on the ship — quick, no discussion, the way you pull a splinter — and found the empty place where it had been. Grisel would be pinging into that silence right now. Calling him a coward out loud, because that was how she prayed.

He let her. It was the only cover he could give her from inside a bag, in a building he was not allowed to know the shape of: to be a hole she couldn't fill, so no one here would think to ask who was on the other end of it.

They moved Eigor deeper into a building he would never be allowed to picture, and the door sealed behind him in its three separate motions, and that was the last the world outside would hear of him for a while. South, on a different water, the runner clawed north through swells that got stranger by the day. Two threads pulling apart on the same dark map, and between them nothing but distance and the time it takes to cross it.

## CHAPTER 13: WHAT REMO KNEW

The Triple Peaks were three black teeth in boiled-looking water, and the runner had no business among them. Tess said so on the approach and kept saying it, which was how Tess prayed. It had taken them the better part of a week to get here — north and east and north again, the water going stranger by the day, until the last stretch turned the color of a bruise and the crew stopped making jokes about it.

Remo's compound was welded onto a slab that had been a parking structure in some drowned century — gun nests, a Skid rack under a tarp doing worse than the Mirlyns' tarp ever had, antennae bristling at a sky that gave nothing back. The water around it was too quiet. Incinerator quiet. The kind of still that meant something under the surface was counting hulls by weight.

A voice hit their comms before a greeting did. *If that's a tax boat, drown where you are. If it's a sale, I don't buy runners. State it.*

"Griselda. I'm looking for Eigor. A Timberite. He was coming to you."

A silence that lasted long enough to be an answer. Then: *West cleat. Keep the virus on the deck where I can see it.*

Remo was a Rhombus who'd kept more of his face than Brox and spent the savings on being unpleasant. Long coat, homemade rifle, a filter mask that was half fashion and half lung. He looked at Brox lashed to the mast, at Grisel, at Tess pricing his compound with her eyes.

"So you're his people," Remo said. "He didn't mention people. He mentioned a chest and a schedule, and then he stopped mentioning anything."

"When," Grisel said.

"Six days back he pinged me from Canary. Said he'd bought a heavy Skid and was coming up the incinerator lane to see me about a job." Remo rolled a shoulder. "He didn't come. Skids don't just sink on that lane — the swarm leaves the light ones alone if you fly them right, and your Timber flew them right or he'd never have made it out of Canary. What eats that lane is the wall people. They ran a boat through the night he was due. When they run, the swarm goes quiet, like even the bots know to hold still. That's what I've got. He was coming to me, he didn't arrive, and the Mirlyns were on the water the same night."

Grisel stood very still while the pieces slid together, because Remo had just handed her the last edges of a thing she'd been building for a week without knowing it.

Remo knew Eigor was coming to him. He did not know why — he'd been careful, in the way careful men are, not to ask what was in the chest. He did not know it was a cure. He did not know a Stillous had paid a crew to steal it. He did not know the crew Varnis paid was the same Onyx skiff Eigor had hit. He had one clean fact: the wall took the lane the night Eigor vanished.

She had the rest. She was the only place all of it lived at once.

She did not tell him. She'd learned that much on the carrier, watching Varnis hold his cards over a dark glass. You did not spread the picture around. The picture was the only thing she owned that everyone else was missing.

"Can you get a message to them," she said. "The wall."

Remo laughed, short and grinding. "I leave a crate at a spot and come back later to see if they left one. That's the whole relationship. I don't talk to Mirlyns. I trade with a door and hope the door's in a mood." Behind the mask, something sharpened. "You want me to leave a crate that says a Howling is asking after a Timberite they may be holding. Understand what that does. Right now, to them, you don't exist. The second I put your name on their doorstep, you exist. And existing is the one thing they kill for reliably."

"What's it cost."

"To leave the crate? Five Gloves, and I still might not come back — in which case the Gloves die with me and you learn nothing." He tilted his head. "That's if I do it. I haven't said I will. A man doesn't get old on this water carrying other people's names to a wall."

Five Gloves. She had ten. She thought about Brox at the mast, fans turning, and about how ten became five became none, and how none of it made a body move again.

"Don't leave the crate," she said. "Not yet."

The ridge where Remo's eyebrow used to be went up. "You come all the way into dead water and then don't spend."

"I came for what you knew. You told me." She was already turning the new shape of it over — the wall had Eigor and the chest and did not know what the chest was worth. "Knocking tells them there's something to hide. I don't want them curious. I want them bored, right up until I know what I'm doing."

Behind her, Tess said quietly, "And do you? Know what you're doing?"

"No," Grisel said. "But I know more than they do. That's a start."

Remo watched her the way you watch a card player who just refused a sure bet. "You didn't grow up trading. You've got fleet in your mouth. Fleet people think honesty is a personality." He almost sounded approving. "Whatever you're building, build it fast. The wall doesn't stay bored. Somebody in there is already asking what's in that box. When they answer their own question, your Timber stops being a courier and starts being a witness. You know what they do with witnesses."

"I'm learning," Grisel said.

She paid him one Glove for what he'd told her, because information you don't pay for gets remembered as a debt, and she wanted no debts in dead water. Nine Gloves. A friend behind a wall. A chest nobody else understood. And, for the first time since Dundy, something under the panic that was almost useful: the cold, plain knowledge that she was standing in the middle of a thing no one else could see all of.

## CHAPTER 14: THE ARITHMETIC

They pulled off the Peaks before the swarm could reconsider them and lay to in open water a half-day south, engine ticking down, the black teeth sinking into the horizon behind them. Nobody suggested they stop closer. Dead water was a place you left as fast as the fuel allowed, and even Vane didn't argue the Gloves it cost.

Grisel laid it out for the crew, because they'd bought in, and a crew that has bought in is owed the real picture or they start inventing worse ones. She sat them at the base of the mast, Brox propped where he was part of it, and counted it off on her claws like a manifest.

"Four people want that chest, or would if they knew to want it. The Stillous on the carrier knows exactly what's inside — a Breaker cure, real, the only working kind. He doesn't have it, and he can't fix Brox without it. The Empire wants it back and lost the trail at the docks. The wall people have it and don't know what it is yet. And Eigor's inside the wall with it, which means the one man who could open his mouth is behind the one door nobody opens."

"And you," Barrett said.

"And me. I know what it is, where it is, and what everyone's missing." She let that sit. "That's what I'm bringing to this. Not Gloves — I've got nine. Not a crew that can storm a Mirlyn port; no crew can. Just that I'm the only one holding the whole picture, and everybody else is trying to make a deal with half of it."

Vane, for once, was quiet a moment before he spoke. "That's not a plan. That's a fact about you."

"It's the start of one." She looked at him, at Tess, at Barrett, at the water. "Every one of those players would pay for the half they're missing. The wall would pay to learn what they're really holding — except telling them makes Eigor a witness, so that door's shut. The Empire would pay to learn it's behind a Mirlyn wall — except pointing Onyx at that wall gets everyone inside it killed, Eigor with them. The Stillous would pay the most. He needs the cure, he can't reach it, and he'll deal with the wall through men like Remo without ever leaving his carrier. Varnis is the lever. He wants it badly enough and he's far enough from it to need me."

Tess wiped her hands slowly. "So you sell Varnis the location of a chest that's behind an obsidian wall. And in exchange he fixes your man and pays us."

"He wants two things to fix Brox. Payment, and the hardware. The hardware is the chest. Put the chest in his hands and the payment's a formality — he'll waive half of it just to hold the only cure on Earth. That's the number that ends with a shuttle. That's your share."

"There's a hole in it," Tess said, because Tess found holes for a living. "You still have to get the chest out of the wall. You just spent a speech explaining nobody gets into the wall."

"I know." Grisel didn't look away from it. "That's the part I don't have. That's the part I go get, before I'm out of Gloves and Brox is one bad repair from a torso that stops twitching." She put her hand on the clamp, the way she used to put it on his coat in a crowd. "But I know what I'm carrying now. A week ago I was a Howling with a bag of arms and no idea why the world was chasing me. Now I know exactly why, and I know I'm the only one who does. On this water, that's almost everything."

Barrett nodded, slow, the way he nodded at bad odds he'd already decided to take. "So where."

Grisel looked southeast, toward a rusted carrier and a Stillous with a glass cylinder and a fondness for unfinished work. "Back to Varnis. Not to sell yet. To find out what he'd give to get his chest back, and what he knows about a wall that I don't. He's older than this ocean. If there's a way in, he's watched somebody try it."

"And if there's not?" Vane asked.

"Then I've still got the one thing nobody can take unless I say it out loud." She checked the clamp a last time and stood, and the crew stood with her — because a payday was a payday, and because somewhere in the last four days, without any of them deciding to, she'd become a person they'd sail with. "Get us moving, Tess. Long way back, and I've got a lot to work out before we're in his water again."

The runner came about and put its ugly nose toward the southeast. Grisel sat with Brox at the mast and did the arithmetic again, the way you worry a knot: nine Gloves, one cure, one wall, one friend behind it, one friend beside her who couldn't speak, and one fact only she could see — worth more than all of it, if she could work out how to spend it without getting them all killed.

The horizon didn't move for her. It never did. But for the first time since the docks, she was the one deciding which part of it to point at.
